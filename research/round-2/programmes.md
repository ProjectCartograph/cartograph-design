## 1. What a programme definition holds, by source

### 1.1 What each source calls the definition

The sources do not share one document. They share a set of questions.

- **MSP** splits a programme's paperwork in three groups: documents that define the why and what (the vision statement, the blueprint, the benefit profiles and the benefits map, the stakeholder map, the programme business case), documents that set strategies, and plans and tools. It starts from a **programme mandate** (a trigger that an idea is worth looking at), moves to a **programme brief** (a first, admitted guess at objectives, benefits, dis-benefits and risks), and then to the full definition. Source: the 2011 edition as the [QA pack](https://www.qa.com/courseware/pcw/pcw-pbpmspp-v4_6.pdf) quotes it. The search summaries I found name the full definition as the "programme definition document", but I could not read it at source.
- **PMI** (4th edition) is organised by performance domains, among them strategy alignment, benefits management, stakeholder engagement, governance and life cycle management, with a life cycle of definition, delivery and closure. Search summaries only. I could not read the standard.
- **APM** describes a programme as a "unique and transient strategic endeavour undertaken to achieve a beneficial change", planned from benefits, with a vision of the future state, a programme board, and an iterative life cycle ([APM page](https://www.apm.org.uk/resources/what-is-project-management/what-is-programme-management/)). Its introduction guide describes a life cycle of concept, definition, delivery and closure, moved through in sub-divisions that serve as approval gates and deliver steps in capability, which it calls tranches ([APM sample, section 2.1](https://www.apm.org.uk/media/lm2fdobv/apm-introduction-to-programme-managementsample.pdf)).
- **GovS 002** defines a programme as a temporary, flexible organisation that coordinates and oversees a set of projects and related work. It requires every programme to have a named senior responsible owner (clause 6.2), says a programme may be phased in tranches and may cover the whole life of a product, service or system (6.3), and says the sponsoring body should review whether benefits and operating performance meet the business case (6.4.9).
- **The Treasury** asks for a **programme business case** built in seven steps and 26 actions, the same five cases as a project but pitched at the set of projects as a whole (chapter 5 and its programme boxes). The detailed cost and benefit analysis, the commercial detail and the delivery detail sit at project level, and the programme case is updated as they firm up.
- **The NAO** does not define a programme. It gives the 18 questions an auditor asks of one, in six groups: purpose, value, governance, delivery resources, set-up of delivery and variation management, and transition. These work as a checklist of what a well-defined programme should be able to answer.
- **Theory of change** sources (UN Peacebuilding Fund; Magenta Book) are not about programmes alone. They add the part the others leave thin: the reasoning from the work to the change.

Notes on the table.

- **Where sources agree** is where a mark runs across most columns: the benefit and how it is measured, what is inside, who is accountable, the link up to strategy, review.
- **MSP's blueprint** is the one element almost no one else asks for. It is a model of the organisation now, in between, and at the end, in four parts: processes, organisation, technology and information. Its useful side effect is the **step change**: outputs introduced in groups so that benefits can start before the end.
- **Tranches** differ by source. GovS 002 defines one as a subdivision of a programme that enables an incremental approach to delivering outputs, outcomes and benefits. The QA pack says an MSP tranche is a milestone where outcomes have been delivered and benefits realisation can start, not just a way to cut up the work. APM ties it to approval gates. The common thread: a tranche is a **decision point with a reason**, not a calendar slice.
- **Benefits are decomposed or kept as one thing.** MSP keeps a profile per benefit (what it is, who feels it, what it depends on) and a map of how they connect. The Treasury keeps a register of all of them, monetised or not. Cartograph has neither noun on purpose (TAXONOMY.md D4).

## 2. What is essential, what is common, what comes later

This is about the order of asking, not size. A small cooperative's programme and a national one both need the first group before anyone can say they have defined a programme. The sources do not say how long each takes, and I do not either.

**Essential: without these it is not yet a programme.** Chosen because several independent source families ask for it, and because a reader cannot judge the programme without it.

1. The change sought, and who is better off (vision, intended outcomes). MSP, APM, Treasury, NAO Q1.
2. What is wrong now, for whom (the case for change). MSP brief, Treasury strategic case, NAO Q1 and Q2.
3. How the benefits are known to have arrived: each measurable, with a way to read it. MSP, Treasury 8.52, NAO Q7, GovS.
4. What is inside it, and what each part contributes. All.
5. Why these parts should produce that change (the reasoning and its assumptions). Treasury, UN, Magenta Book, NAO Q4. MSP's benefits map plays a similar part.
6. One named accountable person, plus a manager and someone who makes the change stick. GovS 6.2, MSP roles, APM, NAO Q8.
7. The authority to proceed, and who can stop it. MSP mandate and brief, GovS gates, Treasury approval points.
8. The main risks and the dependencies on other work. All.

**Common: usually added once the essentials hold, and most programmes need them to be steered.**

- Stakeholders and what each needs (MSP, Treasury, NAO Q3).
- Stages with a stated decision point at the end of each (MSP, APM, GovS, Treasury).
- The value and cost case (Treasury, GovS, NAO Q5 and Q6).
- A named owner for each benefit and a plan for reading it over time (MSP, GovS, Gate 5).
- The route a matter takes up when it cannot be settled here (governance in all of them).
- How the programme will be reviewed, by whom, and what the reviewers decide (Treasury 5.8 to 5.15, NAO Q16, GovS 4.2.3).

**Later: asked when the definition is stable, or when the programme nears an end.**

- A blueprint or target model, with intermediate states (MSP only).
- Options appraised in detail (Treasury steps 3 and 4).
- Economic appraisal with discounting (Treasury, detailed at project level first).
- Change control and re-baselining records (GovS 7.2.5, IPA "Reset").
- Closure criteria and what happens to benefit tracking afterwards (MSP, GovS 6.4.8, Gate 5).
- Lessons learned (NAO Q17, GovS).

**One reversal for long horizons.** Closure criteria and benefit tracking after the work ends look like "later" items. For a programme whose benefits fall years after the work, they are close to essential at the start. The NAO asks whether there is "a strategy in place to deliver the full programme benefits over the long term" (Q7) and whether responsibility for them has been given to operational units (Q18). Both are questions you answer when defining, not when closing. Section 3 says why.

## 3. How a long horizon changes what is asked, and how often it is revisited

A project charter is written once and reread at the gate. A programme with a long life is reread many times, by people who were not there at the start. Six things change.

### 3.1 The definition is a living document, so it needs a review record

The Treasury says a programme business case is not static. It stays in effect from the first project under it to the last, and is reviewed over that time to confirm that the programme is on track and still good value (5.7). The "Gate 0" review is repeated at key decision points (5.10). The business case must be reviewed and validated before each new tranche begins (5.13). UK departments must review it at least annually, and also when one of its project business cases is approved in full (5.15). GovS 002 says new tranches are planned and reviewed before they are authorised, and that the continuing justification is monitored (6.4.6); it also sets an interval for assurance reviews of a whole programme (4.2.3). These intervals are those standards' rules for UK government. They are not numbers for Cartograph to copy. What Cartograph can take from them is the shape: **when the programme was last reviewed, by whom, what they decided, and what is next.**

### 3.2 The question changes from "is it on track?" to "is it still the right thing?"

The NAO's performance question asks whether progress is being measured, "including consideration that the programme is still the right thing to do" (Q16). Its criteria ask whether leadership has clear criteria to judge when corrective action, such as a pause, should be considered. A project can be on time and on budget and no longer worth doing. For a programme, the case for change, the objectives, and the options can all age. The Treasury's Gate 0 review "considers the programme in the wider policy context" and looks at direction and planned outcomes as well as progress of the projects (Table 4).

### 3.3 The vision stays steady while the components change

MSP asks for a short, memorable vision statement that many kinds of stakeholder can understand, drafted early and refined during definition, and kept up to date as things change (QA pack, on the vision theme). The Treasury points the same way: the programme business case sets the case once, and projects underneath it may inherit the strategic case from it, even combining two of their approval stages if it is proportionate (4.3). So the thing that should hold steady is the **aim, the pathway and the benefits**. The components are expected to come and go. The Treasury says projects within a programme may begin at different times, which is why delivery runs in tranches (5.4).

### 3.4 Benefits fall after the work, and someone must own them

A programme exists for benefits. The projects deliver outputs. Often the benefit appears long after. Three sources press the point:

- GovS 002 asks the sponsoring body to ensure a review of whether benefits and operational performance meet the business case, and are likely to keep doing so (6.4.9), and asks the senior responsible owner to agree plans for post-closure reviews (6.4.8).
- Gate Review 5 asks whether a named owner is accountable for both running the service and measuring its benefits over the long term, whether baseline changes go through change control, and whether the assumptions behind the benefits have been tested and updated against actual results. It also asks, for infrastructure, whether a long-term evaluation of social and economic benefits is planned for years into operation. That last item is that gate's expectation for one kind of asset. It is not a rule for Cartograph.
- The IPA's 2023-24 report says only 104 of 227 portfolio projects reported monetised benefits, that many public-value projects are hard to monetise, and that every project leaving the portfolio is expected to have an exit review that considers ongoing tracking of benefits. This shows how often benefits go untracked after the work ends.

MSP says the same in its own words: the benefits theme includes making sure the business can achieve the benefits long term, after the programme ends (QA pack). So the long-horizon version of a benefit asks for: **who owns it, what the baseline is, when it is expected to start showing, how it will be read, and what carries on reading it when the programme has closed.**

### 3.5 Assumptions need a date to be re-tested

The UN Peacebuilding Fund note says assumptions should be made explicit and tested throughout the implementation cycle to see whether they still hold. It also warns that an "if... then" list of project components usually fails to state why change is expected (section 3, "Pro tip"), and that a theory of change explains why, where a logic model describes how (section 1). The Magenta Book says a theory of change should keep being developed over the lifetime of an evaluation as evidence comes in (section 2.2.1), and that where an intervention is large, a set of models focusing on different aspects can be more useful than one (same section). For a long programme this means each assumption deserves a place to say what would tell us it has stopped holding, and a date or event at which someone looks.

### 3.6 Plans get reset, and the reset needs a record

The IPA's annual report uses "Reset" for a significant change to a project's baseline that requires its business case to be refreshed or changed. The NAO asks whether the business case was reassessed at the programme's strategic decision points or on significant change to duration, cost or scope (Q5), and includes several worked examples of a "reset" of a programme and what the reset did and did not solve. GovS 002 requires that changes to an approved baseline are controlled and traceable (7.2.5). I found no source that defines a reset more narrowly. What the sources share is: **the earlier baseline stays visible, the reason is written down, and the approving body decides.**

### 3.7 What this means for how often each part is revisited

I have not found any source that gives one interval for all of this, and I would not invent one. A reasonable reading of the sources is by trigger, not by calendar:

| Part of the definition | Revisited when (as the sources describe it) |
|---|---|
| Aim, vision | At a review of whether the programme is still the right thing (NAO Q16, Gate 0), and at a reset |
| Pathway and assumptions | When evidence arrives, when a tranche ends, when an assumption is shown wrong (UN note, Magenta Book) |
| Components | Whenever a project starts, ends, merges or is cancelled |
| Benefits and indicators | At each reading cycle; owners and baselines at handover to operations (Gate 5) |
| Risks and dependencies | Continuously (GovS 6.4.6), with the major ones before each tranche |
| Governance roles | When people change and at stage boundaries (GovS 6.2: governance may change with the phase) |
| Business case | Before each tranche; after each project case is approved; at a reset (Treasury 5.13, 5.15) |
| Closure criteria | At each review, because they are what keeps a programme from drifting on (MSP closure) |

## 4. What differs from a project charter

Round one found that the charter is a brief that gives a board enough to say yes (../charter-standards.md; TAXONOMY.md D55). Set beside it:

| Matter | Project charter | Programme definition |
|---|---|---|
| What it commits to | Outputs and an end state, within a boundary | A change and its benefits. It delivers nothing itself; the components do |
| What "done" is | Deliverables accepted and handed over | Benefits arriving and holding, then closure with the criteria met |
| Time | Has a start and an end the charter names | A programme has no end date of its own; it ends when its closure conditions are met, and may span the life of a service (GovS 6.3) |
| Scope | In and out, firm | A boundary that moves as components are added and ended |
| The "how" | The plan for the work (milestones, costs, risks) | The reasoning from the components to the change (theory of change, benefits map), not a schedule |
| Plan | Milestones, dates | Tranches with decision points; the components' own plans hold the dates |
| Cost | A budget the project is held to | An overall case; the detailed cost and benefit analysis sits at project level first (Treasury programme box, chapter 8) |
| Governance | A project board, a manager | A named senior responsible owner, a manager, someone who makes the change stick, and a route up for what cannot be settled (GovS 6.2, MSP) |
| Risks | Project risks | Risks to the change, and risks that arise between components |
| Approval | One gate to start, then stage gates | A mandate, a brief, approval of the definition, then a review before each tranche and at intervals |
| Review | At stage gates | Repeated, with the question "is it still the right thing?" |
| After | Handover to operations, then done | The benefits must be handed to someone to carry on reading, and the programme closes only when that holds |
| Who edits it | The project lead, until approved | The programme manager and the sponsor, over years, with others joining and leaving |

The Treasury is clear that a programme does not remove the need for a business case for each project in it (3.9). A programme is also not a big project: cost alone does not make something a programme, and the decision rests on the work needed and how best to manage it (3.8). Cartograph's questions on the New screen already follow this (TAXONOMY.md D32).

## 5. Cartograph against it

I read the Programme schema (`contract/schemas/programme.schema.json`), its guidance (`contract/guidance/en/programme.guidance.json`), its flow (`contract/flows/programme.flow.json`), `docs/TAXONOMY.md` (the Programme sections and D1, D4, D5, D6, D12, D13, D32, D46, D47, D52), `internal/engine/programmechecks.go`, and the programme routes and `copy.programmes` in the UI. I did not run the app. What I say about how a screen reads comes from the code and copy, not from using it.

### 5.1 What it asks

The programme walk has seven steps. Together they ask for:

| Step | What a person writes | Maps to the sources as |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Name; intended change; who gains (optional); source document | Vision; benefit in one sentence; where it is set out |
| Problems | Per problem: who it affects, the situation, the cause, the change that solves it, what is gained, and the gaps it answers | Case for change |
| Alignment | Outcomes (goals), indicators, parent programmes, portfolios | Strategic fit; how benefits are measured; place in a hierarchy |
| Components | Projects and programmes, each with why | What is inside; dependencies |
| How the change happens | Pathway: for each step, the outcome, what must hold first, why, and the assumptions | Theory of change; benefits map in part |
| Risks | Risks, with type, impact, likelihood, mitigation, owner, escalation, and dependencies on other work | Risk register; dependencies |
| Governance | Sponsor, manager, business change manager, lead team, supporting teams, mandate (the decisions and laws behind it), escalation route, notes | Governance; authority; the three MSP roles |

It also holds a stakeholder map scoped to the programme, a rendered charter view, and a results-framework view that reads the pathway as a matrix with the components as outputs. It runs advisory checks on the programme (TAXONOMY.md D6): is anything inside it, is a change stated, is there a pathway (and if not, "this may be a portfolio"), is a lead team named, is each problem tied to a group and a gap, do the components serve the programme's outcomes, is there a loop.

**What it does well, against the sources.**

- It holds the part most tools skip: a pathway where each step says why, and what must be true. It records "and" preconditions only, and gives the reason (D13). This is the Treasury's golden thread and the UN note's "why", and Magenta's assumptions, in one structure.
- It refuses to split benefits into a new noun, and builds them from goal, indicator, group and reading (D4, D8). That fits GovS 002's definition of a benefit as a measurable value or positive impact from an outcome.
- It makes components a graph with loops refused and a critical path derived (D46), which the Treasury asks for at programme level (5.5).
- Its advisory-only checks (D6) suit a definition that is reread over years: a saved programme is never refused because someone edited another file.
- It lets a programme sit in a portfolio and be given a decision of invest, hold or stop with a reason and a date (D32). That covers one part of "is it still the right thing", from outside the programme.
- The three MSP roles (sponsor, manager, business change manager) are asked for, each with a plain gloss (copy: `sponsorHint`, `managerHint`, `changeManagerHint`).

### 5.2 What it lacks

Each item names the source that asks for it. Some were left out deliberately, and I say where TAXONOMY.md already decided.

1. **A review record on the programme.** The sources ask when the programme was last reviewed, by whom, and what was decided (Treasury 5.7 to 5.15; NAO Q16; GovS 6.4.6). A project has an event log and sign-offs (D52). I found nothing like it on a programme. A programme's portfolio can hold a decision about it (D32), but that is the funder's decision about the programme, not the programme's own review of whether it still makes sense. This is the largest gap for a long horizon.
2. **Stages with decision points.** TAXONOMY.md lists "Tranche: nothing" and gives the reason: scheduling belongs to the delivery tool. That is right for dates. The sources, though, describe a tranche as a decision point with a reason (section 1.2), and the Treasury requires a review before each one (5.13). A programme in Cartograph has no way to say "these components are expected to land before the next decision point, and here is what we will look at then". D47 already allows an event to stand in for a date, so a stage could be an event-based decision point, without a calendar. Whether that contradicts the TAXONOMY decision is an open question below.
3. **A reset record.** Nothing says that a programme was re-planned, why, and what the earlier plan was (IPA "Reset"; NAO Q5; GovS 7.2.5). The `notes` map is free text, one per walk step.
4. **Benefit ownership and timing, shown together.** The pieces exist: an indicator names an owner and a source (D44), a goal has a horizon (D26), readings are their own file (D8). I found no programme screen that lists each benefit with its owner, baseline, expected start, and the work that produces it (the sources' benefits register or profile). The business change manager is one role for the whole programme. Gate 5 and the NAO ask for an owner per benefit. This looks derivable from what is held, so it may be a view and not a new field.
5. **Dis-benefits.** MSP, GovS 002 (glossary) and the NAO (Q7, unintended or inequitable consequences) ask for them. I found no place for them in the schema or guidance. Risks cover part of it.
6. **Closure.** No field for the conditions under which the programme ends, nor for what carries the benefit tracking on. MSP names six closure conditions and warns that programmes tend to "drift on". GovS 002 asks for controlled closure and post-closure review plans (6.4.8). The service kind (Operation) can hold the ongoing work, and `Operation.spec.programmes` names it (D2), so the handover target exists. What is missing is the statement that closure is reached when the benefits are being read by that owner.
7. **A target state or interim states.** MSP's blueprint, with its step changes. TAXONOMY.md says out of scope: Cartograph captures and does not design the future state. The pathway's intermediate outcomes cover part of it. I would leave this alone.
8. **Programme-level cost and funding.** The Treasury's programme case has a financial case, with detail at project level first. Cartograph holds funding on the project and the portfolio's budgets, and TAXONOMY.md calls the business case "partial and deliberate". I did not find a programme-level cost view. Whether to add one is a decision for the owner of the product, and the lean-core rule (memory: lean core, ports for extras) argues for caution.
9. **A date or trigger on each assumption.** Assumptions are held and linked from the pathway (`assumes`). I found nothing for "what would tell us this has stopped holding" or "look again by". Section 3.5 says long programmes need it. A timing, in the D47 sense, could serve.
10. **Assurance plan.** GovS 002 (4.2.3) and the Treasury (5.12) ask for a plan for what will review the programme and when. Related to item 1.

### 5.3 What it asks that it should not (or asks in the wrong place)

1. **The lead team is required and the accountable person is not.** The schema requires `aim` and `leadTeam` (`required` on `spec`). GovS 002 says every programme shall be under a named senior responsible owner (6.2). MSP names the SRO as the vision's author. The sponsor field is optional. A programme can be saved with a team and no one answerable for its benefits. This is a contract question: making a field required may break saved programmes (VERSIONING.md says what a release promises; I did not check this case), so the safer route may be a check that advises, in line with D6.
2. **The components step uses a dependency test for a membership question.** The flow's hint reads: "The projects and programmes this one depends on. Add one only if this work cannot finish without it." A programme's components are in it because they contribute to the change. A programme does not wait on its projects in that sense: it is made of them. The sources describe a programme as coordinating them. D46 chose "depends on" for both projects and programmes, for good reasons about direction and shared components, but the wording carries the project's meaning into the programme. Also, `copy.programmes.insideHint` says a component is added by opening it and choosing this programme, which describes the older direction (D1, before D46). The two hints disagree. I did not run the app to see which one a person meets.
3. **The problems step is heavy for a programme.** Per problem it asks for groups, a situation, a cause, a change, a gain, and cited gaps. The sources ask for the case for change once. D45 makes the problem agree with its gaps, which suggests the gap register already holds most of this. This is my judgement from reading the flow, not from watching anyone use it. A walk-first approach (cartograph-ui#136) would likely ask the aim and one problem first, and leave the rest to the full workspace.
4. **Risks use the full project shape.** Each programme risk carries type, impact, likelihood, mitigation, owner, escalation and a dependency edge. Sources ask a programme for risks to the change and between components. The shape is shared on purpose (D6), and it is optional per field, so this is mild.

### 5.4 Where Cartograph's decisions and the sources already agree

- **No Benefit kind** (D4): consistent with the discipline's definition, as above.
- **Programme versus portfolio** (D32): consistent with the Treasury's four-level hierarchy of policy, portfolio, programme and project, where each sets objectives against the level above (3.11), and with the Treasury's point that a project's cost alone does not make it a programme (3.8).
- **Checks that advise** (D6): consistent with a living definition.
- **Sub-programmes** (D5 resolved by D32, `spec.programmes`): consistent with GovS 002 and PMI both allowing programmes inside programmes.

## 6. The Diátaxis needs of a programme manager and a programme sponsor

Diátaxis sorts what people need along two axes: action or cognition, and study or work ([start here](https://diataxis.fr/start-here/)). The same map works as a lens on what a person does with the product. A **programme manager** is a doer who must also teach themselves, and returns to the programme over years. A **programme sponsor** is mostly a reader and a decider, who needs the programme in front of them briefly, now and then, and who must trust it.

The user tests found that people could do the walk with one question per screen and plain words, and gave up when faced with a full screen (COMMON.md). The needs below assume walk-first.

| | Programme manager | Programme sponsor |
|---|---|---|
| **Study** (tutorial: learn by doing, one route) | A first programme, walked from a handful of related projects: give it an aim, name the problem, list the components, write the first pathway step with its reason. The learner finishes with something that reads as a programme. | A short guided read of a finished example: what the programme is for, what it is judged on, who is answerable, what it asks of you at the next review. The learner finishes knowing what a good one looks like and what to ask. |
| **Work** (how-to: a task, for someone competent) | Add a component. Record why a step on the pathway holds. Write down an assumption and when to look at it again. Give a benefit an owner. Record a tranche review. Record a reset. Hand benefit tracking to the business change manager. Close the programme. Move a project from one programme to another. | Check whether the programme still makes sense. Approve the next tranche. Ask for a review. Stop or hold a programme from the portfolio. Name a new sponsor when the role changes hands. |
| **Look up** (reference: facts, in the shape of the thing) | Every field of the programme, in the walk's order, with what the checks look for. The three accountable roles and what each does. The meaning of each pathway word. The advice lines the programme shows and what clears each. | The one-page view: aim, benefits and how far they have come, what is inside, the open risks, the last review and its decision. A glossary of the words on it. |
| **Understand** (explanation: why) | Why a programme is not a bigger project or a portfolio. Why a pathway has a reason on every step, and "and" not "or". Why benefits are not a separate thing. Why a tranche is a decision point. What a reset is and why it is recorded, not hidden. Why closure needs a condition. | Why the programme keeps asking whether it is still the right thing. Why benefits appear after the work and who has to keep reading them. What a sponsor is accountable for, and what is not theirs. |

Three points on use.

1. **The sponsor's page is not the manager's page.** A sponsor's job, from every source, is to decide and to answer for benefits. They need a short read-only view and a small number of actions (approve, hold, ask). The charter view is the closest thing today. A decision on what the sponsor sees is a UI question for the UI repository. I note it because the test result says "too much on every screen" and a sponsor is the user most likely to meet the full screen by mistake.
2. **A manager returns to a programme over years and forgets.** The how-to set above is mostly the "revisit" tasks in section 3.7, not the first-time ones. If the guide covers only the walk, it serves the first afternoon and not the following years.
3. **Study versus work for the same person.** Someone who is new to programmes (a project officer made a programme manager) needs the tutorial and the explanation about what makes a programme different from a project. The same person a year later needs the how-tos. Keep the four kinds apart, as Diátaxis says, and link between them.

## Open questions

1. **Tranches.** TAXONOMY.md says a tranche is scheduling and out of scope. The sources, and the Treasury's rule of a review before each one, describe it as a decision point. Is a "stage" of a programme (a named decision point, with the components expected before it, and a recorded review outcome, and no calendar dates) inside the capture boundary? If so it would be built from D47's event timings and D52's sign-off line.
2. **Review record.** Should a programme have an event log and sign-offs as a project does (D52)? A review is an event on the programme; its outcome (continue, change, hold, stop) is a decision. The portfolio's decisions (D32) record a funder's view. Is the programme's own view a separate thing?
3. **Reset.** Does it need its own record, or is a change set on the programme (the every-change-through-a-change-set rule in the project memory) already the record, so that only a reason and a name for it are missing?
4. **Required fields.** Should the accountable person become required, or stay advised? Changing `required` is a contract change.
5. **Components wording.** Which of the two hints is live in the app today, and should a programme's components step use "contributes to the change" in place of "depends on"?
6. **Benefit register as a view.** Can it be derived from goals, indicators, readings and owners alone, or does a per-benefit owner and expected start need a new field? I did not check whether the framework view or any screen already lists these together.
7. **MSP 5th edition.** The MSP column rests on the 2011 edition through a training pack. The 5th edition's terms for the vision, blueprint, benefits and the definition document, and whether it now asks for anything on long horizons, are unverified. A copy of the official guidance is needed.
8. **PMI Standard for Program Management.** Unread. Its roadmap, its benefits register, and its governance domain are known to me only from search summaries, one of which says the standard names a roadmap and does not explain it.
9. **IPA benefits guidance.** The 2017 and 2021 guides were not read. The treatment of benefit handover certificates, mentioned in a search summary, is unverified.
10. **APM Body of Knowledge, 7th and 8th editions.** Unread. The 8th is said to extend the life cycle from the strategic case through benefits delivery. Not confirmed.
11. **Theory of change over a long life.** I found guidance that assumptions be tested and the theory kept developing, but no source that says how often, or how to record a revision. The UNDG companion guidance and Vogel's review would be the next places to look.
12. **Cartograph in practice.** I read the code and copy and did not run the programme screens. Whether people meet the heavy problems step or the conflicting component hints as I describe is untested.
