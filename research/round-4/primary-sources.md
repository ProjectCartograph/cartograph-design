# M1: primary sources, read at source

Round 4 note for milestone M1 (issue #1). Date: 2026-10-18. Read-only research; no requirement or decision record was changed. Verdicts in section 3 are for the owner to accept or not.

Earlier rounds could not fetch pmi.org, iso.org, the AXELOS and PeopleCert pages, the OECD site or parts of gov.uk, and relied on search summaries and secondary pages. This round read the same pages through headless Chromium (and a PDF-to-text step for PDFs). Where only a landing page, a publisher's summary or a committee page was public, that is what was read, and this note says so. Nothing behind a paywall, a login or a licence was opened. Where a site served a bot challenge instead of a page (the Teal Book at projectdelivery.gov.uk, inee.org), I stopped there and did not look for a way round it.

The extracted texts were kept in a working folder outside this repository. Paragraph and section numbers below are the sources' own.

## 2. What the sources say on the points the requirements rest on

### 2.1 The case and its stages

- **One case, grown through stages.** The Treasury's 2026 guidance builds a project business case in three steps, the strategic outline case, the outline business case and the full business case, each assured by a gate before the approving authority decides (chapter 4, paragraphs 4.13 to 4.24). After approval the case stays in use as a delivery document and a reference point (4.25). Departments review a programme business case at least annually and before each new tranche (5.13 to 5.15). This is the Green Book's five case model; it was already in rounds 1 and 3.
- **Five questions at any scale.** The Gate 5 workbook says its questions follow the Five Case Business Case Model, with add-ons by project type ([Gate 5](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gate-review-5-operations-review-and-benefit-realisation/gate-review-5-operational-review-and-benefits-realisation), introduction).
- **Stages are not one fixed ladder.** The IPA guide says no single mapping ties each benefits practice to a project stage, that the practices are not strictly sequential, and that they repeat in each stage as certainty grows (2017 guide, 7.2, 7.3). NISTA's assurance guide calls its five benefits stages interrelated and says they should be "pursued concurrently", with expected maturity differing by gate (introduction). It also records that MSP uses four stages, not five (footnote 3).
- **Life-cycle neutrality.** ISO 21502 says it applies to any life cycle model and any delivery approach, including agile ([committee page](https://committee.iso.org/sites/tc258/home/projects/published/iso-21502.html), scope note). The IPA guide says the same of its practices (2.6). PMI's standards catalogue describes the 8th edition as for work that is predictive, adaptive "or anywhere in between" ([PMI standards](https://www.pmi.org/standards), PMBOK Guide entry).
- **PMI's performance domains changed.** The 7th edition named eight (stakeholder, team, development approach and life cycle, planning, project work, delivery, measurement, uncertainty). The 8th names seven (governance, scope, schedule, finance, stakeholders, resources, risk) and six principles. Neither list was read inside the standard; both come from PMI's own pages.

### 2.2 Decisions and gates

- **Decision outcomes.** At the strategic outline case the approving authority may approve, change the scope, require different options, order a pilot or more feasibility work, or "postpone or abandon" the project if it is too risky, too costly or too ambitious (HM Treasury guidance, 4.14). Business cases exist so that proposals that are not value for money can be halted or reset (3.3). The list has no outcome called approve with conditions; conditions appear elsewhere, as conditions on funding letters (10.24) and as review points set by conditions such as the end of a tranche (5.14).
- **Reviews advise; they do not approve.** NISTA says the Gate Review process gives independent guidance to the senior responsible owner and teams, that a gate is a snapshot at a point in time, and that having a review "does not replace" an audit opinion on risk management (Gate 5 workbook, introduction). It adds that the topics and evidence are indicative, not prescriptive.
- **A repeating review.** Gate 5 can repeat in mid-life for an operating service; a final one covers closing the current service contract and lessons. The operational business owner decides when later reviews happen (Gate 5 workbook, "Operations Review and Benefits Realisation").
- **PMI portfolio decisions (2013 text).** Authorising a portfolio is a formal act that records governance decisions and returns the funding and resources of terminated or cancelled components to a pool (Standard for Portfolio Management, 3rd edition, 5.4.1 and 5.4.3). The portfolio manager guides the selection, prioritisation, balancing and termination of components (1.8).

### 2.3 Programmes and their benefits

- **PMI's first definitions (2006).** A program is a group of related projects managed in a coordinated way to obtain benefits and control not available from managing them singly. Three themes run through every program: benefits management, stakeholder management and governance. The papers add that a large project split into related projects, with explicit management of the benefits, "becomes a program" ([program paper](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/new-project-management-institute-standard-development-8014), foundations section). This is the claim round 2 had from a search summary; it is now confirmed at source for the 2006 first edition.
- **The 5th edition.** PMI's product page says it states eight principles and adds a new performance domain, Collaboration ([5th edition page](https://www.pmi.org/standards/program-management-fifth-edition), overview). Nothing more was public.
- **ISO 21503** gives high-level descriptions of terms, concepts, prerequisites, practices, roles and responsibilities, and says it does not give processes, methods or tools ([committee page](https://committee.iso.org/sites/tc258/home/projects/published/iso-21503.html), scope).
- **MSP** (syllabus headings only): programme principles, themes and processes; a lifecycle from identifying and defining a programme through delivery phases, realising benefits and closing; governance and control; benefits realisation; roles; tailoring ([PeopleCert Foundation page](https://www.peoplecert.org/browse-certifications/project-programme-and-portfolio-management/MSP-6/msp-foundation-5th-edition-2930)). The syllabus names the senior responsible owner and benefits manager as roles.
- **The UK definition of a benefit.** "The measurable improvement resulting from an outcome perceived as an advantage by one or more stakeholders", from the Cabinet Office, quoted by the IPA guide (3.1). Benefits are not the change itself, are judged differently by different stakeholders, and a benefit to one party may be a disbenefit to another. The guide separates disbenefits from risks (3.5): a disbenefit is a negative consequence that will happen; a risk may or may not.
- **Projects and programmes.** The IPA guide's footnote 2 to paragraph 2.6 says the traditional split (projects deliver outputs, programmes combine them into outcomes) gives way, in benefits-led practice, to projects delivering outcomes and realising benefits in their own right, except enabling projects. NISTA uses "project" for both and notes the old split (executive summary, footnote 2).

### 2.4 Portfolios and their decisions

- **Definitions.** PMI 2013: a portfolio is a collection of programs, projects or operations managed as a group to achieve strategic objectives, and the components need not depend on one another or share objectives; they can be measured, ranked and prioritised; portfolios and programs can run longer than projects, which have a defined start and end (3rd edition, 1.2). Portfolio management is where a governing body decides the direction of a group of components (1.3).
- **ISO 21504** gives guidance on the principles of project and programme portfolio management, for any organisation, and not on financial portfolios ([committee page](https://committee.iso.org/sites/tc258/home/projects/published/iso-21504.html), scope). Nothing on criteria, decisions or stopping was public.
- **MoP** (syllabus headings only): strategic alignment, prioritisation by objectives, available resources and other factors, risk, benefits management, governance, and a stepwise approach to planning and delivering portfolios ([PeopleCert Foundation page](https://www.peoplecert.org/browse-certifications/project-programme-and-portfolio-management/MoP-4/mop-foundation-2644)).
- **Benefits at portfolio level.** The IPA guide says a portfolio office can give a consistent benefits approach and lists what to weigh: prioritising projects by expected benefit and weighted strategic objectives, and the relation between initiative benefits and portfolio benefits, in terms of attribution and contribution (section 10, 10.1 to 10.4).

### 2.5 Evaluation criteria

- **The six criteria and their questions** (OECD 2019, section 2.3): relevance (is it doing the right things), coherence (how well does it fit with other work), effectiveness (is it achieving its objectives), efficiency (how well are resources used), impact (what difference does it make) and sustainability (will the benefits last). Coherence is the one new criterion (paragraph 9, Box 3).
- **What they are for.** Each is a different lens on the intervention and together they describe desired attributes (paragraphs 10 and 11). They serve accountability and learning (12), are also used for monitoring, results management, planning and design (13), and can be used before, during or after (14). "Intervention" covers a project, programme, policy, strategy, institution and more, run by governments, firms or non-governmental bodies (Box 1).
- **Principles of use** (paragraph 15). One: apply the criteria thoughtfully, in context. Two: use depends on purpose and the criteria "should not be applied mechanistically"; time and resources may differ by criterion. Paragraph 6 records that covering too many criteria and questions was a particular problem found in the consultation.
- **2021 guidance, chapter 3.** The criteria are not a methodology and not the goals of an intervention. Users may be tempted to apply all six regardless of context, but the better approach is to choose deliberately by asking if a criterion matters and if it can be answered. It warns against cherry-picking easy or flattering criteria and says not to shy away from impact and coherence. Evaluations often draw an overall conclusion, sometimes with a numerical score and weights, and some institutions name a dominant, knock-out criterion; if it fails, the intervention is unsuccessful or, ex ante, not funded.
- **Relevance has a time dimension.** The 2019 definition says the objectives and design respond to needs and continue to do so if circumstances change (relevance, definition and Box 2). Design includes the theory of change, risk analysis and context analysis.
- **Counterfactual and alternatives.** Efficiency is judged against feasible alternatives in the context; the Box 5 note says a valid cost-benefit analysis needs the pertinent counterfactuals.
- **Groups and equity.** Effectiveness covers differential results across groups and does not assume equity is an objective (Box 4).
- **Results levels.** Effectiveness looks at objectives and results along the results chain, which are more closely attributable; impact looks at higher-level effects (Boxes 4 and 6). The 2021 guidance adds that managers and evaluators should make a clear distinction between input, output, outcome and impact (chapter 4, linkage between effectiveness and impact). It also says relevance work should check whether objectives are poorly phrased or "focused on activities or inputs" and whether indicators meet SMART tests (chapter 4, relevance).
- **Evaluability.** The 2021 guidance asks, early, whether data and access allow the evaluation, and suggests working with stakeholders to document decisions or rebuild missing baselines (executive summary; chapter 3 on context).
- **Sustainability.** Net benefits continue, across financial, economic, social, environmental and institutional capacities (sustainability, Box 7).
- **ISO 21513:2026** (scope only): guidance on evaluating projects and programmes after closure, focusing on achievement of objectives, actual outcomes, actual realisation of benefits, whether they will likely meet expectations in future, and how effective governance and management were. It does not cover evaluation before or during delivery.

### 2.6 Benefits after delivery

- **Benefits management spans before, during and after.** The IPA guide: it runs through the whole life cycle, not only investment decisions; identification starts before the project; measurement continues after close (3.3, 3.4). NISTA: benefits management is one of the few elements that spans the whole life cycle (executive summary).
- **Reporting after closure.** Benefits reports (usually by the portfolio or programme office, signed off by the board) continue until most benefits are realised or are performing as expected on a steady basis (IPA 7.78). Minimum report content: title and measure, baseline and target, current and previous performance, status with commentary, and owner (7.77). For wider benefits that cannot be tied to one initiative, the budget and timing are agreed with an evaluation team, which studies them at agreed later points (7.79).
- **A reporting rhythm that matches the data.** Do not report monthly if the data come yearly (7.69).
- **Handover.** A Benefits Handover Certificate is a formal sign-off between the project and the benefit owner, with the date and any terms such as reporting duties (9.8). Gate 4 asks for a list of benefit owners, a benefits tracking tool and a plan for post-implementation reviews; Gate 5 for the review itself (NISTA, Gate 4 and 5 sections).
- **Gate 5.** Handover from the senior responsible owner to the operational business owner typically happens within a year of the service starting, and the business owner is then responsible for the benefits. Gate 5 confirms accountability, a lessons mechanism, and that earlier recommendations were closed (Gate 5 workbook, purpose and timing). Its questions include unanticipated benefits, the size of disbenefits and the net benefits position once disbenefits are counted (NISTA, Gate 5).
- **Treasury.** Practitioners should develop a benefits register describing how each benefit is monitored, with its initial baseline and an owner responsible for its realisation; the register is iterative and kept up into operations (HM Treasury guidance, 11.20 to 11.22). It should hold all benefits, whether monetisable or not (8.52). A benefit's baseline here is its value under business as usual, in money, numbers or words, beside its category, beneficiary and metric (7.24).
- **PMI** has a Benefits Realization Management guide (December 2018) that describes a life cycle from mission and strategy to deliverables and success measurement; I read the page only.

### 2.7 Roles

- **IPA guide, section 8.** The senior responsible owner owns the business case, is accountable for identifying, quantifying, managing and communicating benefits, and is typically accountable for realising them, though that may be devolved to the programme director in major projects. Programme directors are responsible day to day; economists and finance staff value benefits and document assumptions. An example RACI is in Annex C (not read in full). Benefit owners in business as usual take over realisation. The principles require evidence that the individuals assigned roles have accepted them (5.1).
- **Assurance.** Reviews are independent of delivery and advise the senior responsible owner (Gate 5 workbook, introduction).
- **PMI.** The 2013 portfolio standard names the portfolio manager and the PMO (1.8, 1.9). The 8th edition lists leading accountably among its six principles (PMBOK page, what you'll learn). Roles in the programme and project standards were not reachable.
- **ISO 21505** is aimed at governing bodies and senior management who decide on governance, and at sponsors, steering committees, portfolio owners and project offices ([scope](https://committee.iso.org/sites/tc258/home/projects/published/iso-21505.html)).

## 4. What the requirements do not hold

Each item is a candidate for the owner, not a change.

1. **Unanticipated benefits.** NISTA Gate 5 asks for a list of benefits nobody foresaw, and a plan to keep tracking them. B1 to B6 only cover benefits planned in advance.
2. **Who receives a benefit.** NISTA Gate 1 asks that benefits be categorised at least by recipient stakeholder, and the guide has an appendix on categories. E3 (Should) asks who is affected for results; no requirement asks it of each benefit.
3. **Which benefits will be pursued, and why.** NISTA Gates 1 to 3 ask for benefits in priority order, a reason, and the subset chosen for realisation with proof of capability to deliver it. B1 holds no priority.
4. **Risks to benefits.** NISTA names a risk register that includes risks to the benefits being realised at Gates 2 to 5. A1 to A6 do not link a risk to a benefit.
5. **Net position after disbenefits.** Gate 5 asks for it. B2 records disbenefits but not the net.
6. **Lasting.** OECD's sustainability and the PMBOK 8 principle on integrating sustainability both ask whether benefits last, across financial, social, environmental and institutional capacity. B6 does not carry g2 L2's "what is needed so the benefit lasts".
7. **Still relevant.** OECD relevance, and IPA guide 7.7, ask periodically whether objectives remain relevant and achievable as circumstances change. R3's "reminder to ask if still needed" covers services only.
8. **Overlap with other work.** OECD coherence, internal and external, asks whether other interventions support, undermine or duplicate this one. D9 and A4 look upward and sideways at dependencies; none asks about duplication.
9. **Evaluability.** The 2021 guidance asks early whether the work can be evaluated, given data and access. E5 says proportion is recorded, not that evaluability is.
10. **Reporting rhythm.** IPA guide 7.69: set a reporting frequency to the data. M1 has "how often" but no check against when data arrive.
11. **Evaluating the management itself.** ISO 21513 (scope) includes the effectiveness of governance and management of the project or programme in a post-closure evaluation. B6 asks about delivery and lessons, not a judgement on governance.
12. **Handover terms.** The IPA certificate includes the handover date and any terms (reporting duties). B5 records acceptance only.
13. **Baseline has two meanings.** See D8. The Treasury and IPA also tell teams to re-baseline a benefit measure before delivery. The requirements use the one word for two things.
14. **A sourcing note on the Green Book.** Rounds 2 and 3 cite "Green Book 11.20 to 11.22" and "8.52" for the benefits register. In 2026 those paragraphs are in the Treasury's Guidance on Developing Business Cases, not in the Green Book 2026 (where 8.52 is about transport and 4.14 defines business as usual). The content matches; the citation target moved. The same may hold for other business-case paragraph numbers.
15. **PMI's current editions.** The program standard is now in its 5th edition (the brief and rounds 2 and 3 name the 4th). The PMBOK Guide is in its 8th edition with seven domains and six principles, not the 7th edition's eight and twelve. Requirements do not cite PMI editions, but TAXONOMY.md quotes PMI, and its wording should be checked against the edition it was taken from.

## 5. What still needs a person with access

- PMI: The Standard for Program Management (5th), The Standard for Portfolio Management (4th), the PMBOK Guide 8th and The Standard for Project Management (members' downloads); the Benefits Realization Management and Governance practice guides.
- ISO 21500, 21502, 21503, 21504, 21505, 21513 and 21506: clause-level text, terms and contents (iso.org/obp behind its terms gate, or a purchased copy). Of most value: any ISO 21502 or 21503 clause on benefits, stage decisions and closure; ISO 21504 on criteria and stopping; ISO 21513 in full.
- MSP 5th edition and MoP / PRINCE2 Portfolio Management: the manuals.
- The Teal Book chapters 12 and 19 and the benefits practitioner workbook, on projectdelivery.gov.uk, to a person who can pass its bot check, or through any later copy on gov.uk.
- The IPA's 2017 guide Annexes A to C, and a check that the gov.uk original is unchanged since 2017.
