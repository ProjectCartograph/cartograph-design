# G1: the Green Book and the business case, and what other domains say

Brief: `brief-g1.md`, with `COMMON-GREENFIELD.md` and `../COMMON.md`.
The owner's later addition is applied: every criterion below says which
domains' sources support it and whether it is domain-neutral or tied to
one domain.

Focus: the structure of a decision to invest over time. What is decided
at each stage, what must be known by then, what is deferred, who
approves, how options are generated and compared, and how the case stays
alive after approval.

Two warnings about reading the UK sources. First, the Green Book is
written for UK public spending and says so (its scope is "UK society",
6.5). I take its structure and reasoning, and I do not take its numbers
(discount rates, time horizons, thresholds, optimism bias percentages)
into any criterion. Second, the Green Book 2026 and the business case
guidance changed from earlier editions that I know from memory. I cite
only paragraphs I read.

## 1. What the sources say

### 1.1 The UK: Green Book and business case guidance

**Appraisal is one part of a cycle.** The Green Book says a proposal
runs through rationale, objectives, appraisal, monitoring, evaluation and
feedback (ROAMEF, 2.2). It covers the first three. Monitoring and
evaluation are in the Magenta Book (2.3), and delivery is in the Teal
Book. The Green Book keeps appraisal separate from evaluation (1.10) but
tells authors to plan evaluation from the outset.

**The four steps of an appraisal (2.8, Table 1).**
1. Rationale and objectives.
2. Generate options and appraise a longlist.
3. Appraise a shortlist.
4. Identify the preferred option.

**Rationale and objectives have five parts (2.11, 4.1).** Case for
change, theory of change, business as usual, SMART objectives, strategic
fit. Notes that matter for a tool:
- Business as usual "does not mean doing nothing" (4.15). It has its own
  costs, benefits and risks, and it must be described fairly. Over- or
  under-stating it tilts the decision (4.18). Do not normalise it to zero
  (4.19).
- A theory of change must show its assumptions, and decision makers must
  agree them (4.12).
- Objectives should be few (4.23), and should be outcomes where possible,
  not outputs (4.25), so that more than one way to deliver stays open.
  Outputs are fine where a higher level already settled the need (4.26).
- Strategic fit means fit with the programme, portfolio or policy above,
  and with other work by the organisation and by others (4.27).

**The policy hierarchy and the golden thread (3.1 to 3.10).** Policies,
portfolios, programmes and projects. Each level sets its objectives with
reference to the one above. The alignment is the "golden thread" (3.5),
and the business case guidance says authors "must" describe it (3.12 of
that guidance). A project's appraisal need not re-justify its programme
(3.6). A project may have no benefit of its own and still matter to a
programme (3.9).

**The Five Case Model (3.12 to 3.17; guidance chapter 2).** Strategic,
economic, commercial, financial, management. They are "five distinct but
closely linked perspectives on the same proposal", developed together
and refined in light of each other (3.15). There is no need to invent a
sixth case for environmental, legal or ethical matters. They go in as
objectives, critical success factors or costs and benefits (3.16).

**Stages of a project business case (guidance chapter 4).**
- Stage 0, strategic assessment. It produces a scoping document and is
  reviewed by the approving authority (4.8, 4.9). The document records
  the mandate, the strategic fit, the brief, the plan for the business
  case process, and any feasibility and risk potential results (6.4).
  Annex B gives a template. It names the senior responsible owner and
  the approving authority and records which type of case was chosen and
  why.
- Strategic Outline Case. The scoping phase: case for change, longlist,
  shortlist, a "preferred way forward" (3.17). The approving authority
  may approve, change the scope, require different options, commission a
  pilot, or postpone or abandon (4.14).
- Outline Business Case. The planning phase: detailed appraisal of the
  shortlist, a "preferred option", outline commercial, financial and
  management arrangements (4.16). Approval needs confidence in value for
  money and in propriety, regularity and feasibility (4.19).
- Full Business Case. The procurement phase: confirms the case after
  supplier offers and sets out the final arrangements (4.21 to 4.24).
  Contracts are signed only after this approval (4.24).
- Each case is only part-complete at each stage (the guidance's Figures
  3 to 5 show all five cases growing together). The SOC "must also begin"
  commercial, financial and management thinking (4.11).
- Stages can be combined where a programme has already made the strategic
  case (4.3 to 4.5), or replaced by a one-stage justification case for
  small, low-risk, pre-competed purchases (3.17, 3.22). Combining stages
  "does not mean" skipping steps (4.4).
- The business case is "living": it is revisited and updated as the
  proposal develops (3.19). After approval it is the reference point for
  logging material changes, and the benefits and risk registers keep
  being updated (4.25).
- Programme business cases are never "finished": they are reviewed and
  validated before each tranche starts (5.7, 5.13), and a department
  reviews them at least annually (5.15).
- Assurance sits outside the author. Gate Reviews 0 to 5 run before each
  approval, and Gate 5 repeats through the life of the service to confirm
  benefits are being achieved (Table 2, 3.26). The guidance says gates
  are not the only assurance and that assurance should be proportionate
  (4.7).

**Approval and what follows (Treasury Approvals Process).**
- A decision is to approve, approve with conditions, or reset (5.2). It
  must be in writing and cannot be inferred from silence (5.4).
- A reset is a substantial change to objectives, approach, outputs,
  schedule or whole-life cost (5.17). A reset may need an updated case
  and fresh approval (5.18).
- Evaluation plans: business cases are "unlikely to receive approval"
  without a proportionate one (2.14). Plans and outputs are logged in a
  public registry (5.15).
- Publication and reporting duties apply to the largest programmes
  (5.12, 5.13). These are UK-specific.
- Agreement on the type of case is made with the approver at the start
  (guidance 3.22, TAP 2.5). Early engagement is a stated principle
  ("right first time", TAP 1.3).

**Options (Green Book chapter 5).**
- Options generation and longlist appraisal are "often overlooked" and
  must be documented, with reasons for each option going forward or not
  (5.3, 5.4).
- Critical success factors are the attributes an option must have (5.5).
  Constraints can be among them (5.6).
- Dependencies and unintended consequences, including gaming of targets,
  are checked at longlist (5.11, 5.12).
- The recommended technique is the options framework filter (5.13). Five
  categories: scope, solution, delivery, implementation, funding (5.25).
  Each choice in each category is rated green, amber or red against the
  critical success factors, with a short written reason (5.26, 5.27).
  Green and amber choices are combined into options (5.35). Structured,
  facilitated workshops and explicit reasons are "essential features"
  (5.22, 5.23). Proportionality still applies.
- The shortlist should include business as usual, a do minimum, a
  preferred way forward, and a more and a less ambitious variant
  (5.18). Business as usual always goes through to the shortlist as the
  benchmark (2.16). An option that does not meet the objectives does not
  proceed (5.9).
- The Green Book recommends against simple weighting and scoring
  (multi-criteria analysis) because it "lacks an objective basis" (5.15).
  It allows multi-criteria decision analysis with an expert facilitator
  (5.14).
- The preferred way forward at longlist may not be the preferred option
  after shortlist appraisal (5.20).

**Shortlist and judgement (Green Book chapter 6, chapter 9).**
- Value for money is a "balanced judgement" over seven factors
  (Box 1, 2.6): performance against objectives and critical success
  factors, monetisable and unmonetisable costs and benefits, public
  financial impact, distribution, and risk and uncertainty.
- A benefit-cost ratio threshold must not decide. A ratio below one can
  still be value for money, and metrics may only compare options for the
  same objectives (6.98 to 6.100).
- Costs and benefits are classified as monetisable, quantifiable or
  qualitative, and the guidance says to monetise where possible, quantify
  next, and describe last (6.26 to 6.28). Effort should be proportionate
  (6.29).
- Affordability is a different question from value for money. The
  financial case looks at cost to the public purse in nominal terms. The
  economic case looks at society in real, discounted terms (Table 9).
- Risk and uncertainty: give a central estimate and a range (6.68),
  consider scenarios (6.74), and show sensitivity and switching values
  for the preferred option (6.102 to 6.104).
- Optimism bias is a "demonstrated systematic tendency" and must be
  adjusted for explicitly (6.78, 6.79). The size should come from the
  organisation's own record of forecast against outturn (6.80, 10.5).
- Results are shown in an appraisal summary table with the same columns
  for every option, so that a ratio does not stand alone (9.3 to 9.6).

**Supplementary guidance on optimism bias.** Generic percentages exist
for use only when an organisation has no evidence of its own (1.2, 3.1).
Start from the upper bound, reduce only as contributory factors are
managed, and only on "clear and tangible evidence", independently
verified (3.17). If bias is still high, approval should be withheld or
given on qualified terms. High bias may be acceptable at the outline
stage and not at the full stage (3.19). Where there is no data for
benefits, ask how far benefits can fall short before the proposal stops
being worthwhile, and how likely that is (4.1).

**Benefits and their management.**
- Each benefit: category (cash-releasing, non-cash-releasing,
  quantifiable, qualitative), beneficiary, metric, baseline (guidance
  7.24).
- A benefits register with an owner per benefit, a baseline, and a note
  on how it will be monitored. It is attached to the business case and
  kept up to date into operations (guidance 11.21, 11.22).
- Benefits management is "a structured way to identify, plan, track and
  verify" (11.20).
- Objectives are not benefits. The guidance warns against confusing them
  (7.7).

**Monitoring and evaluation (Green Book chapter 10; guidance 11.33 to
11.42).**
- Monitoring tracks delivery. Evaluation assesses design, delivery and
  outcome. They serve learning and accountability (10.3).
- Three types: process, impact, and value for money (10.4).
- An evaluation plan is made "as soon as" the preferred option is
  identified and is in place before final approval (10.7). The cost of
  monitoring and evaluation sits in the economic and financial cases
  (10.8). There is no fixed share of budget for it, and novel proposals
  need more (10.9).
- When a proposal is later evaluated, the evaluation goes back to the
  original appraisal: were objectives met, did the theory of change
  hold, did costs and benefits happen, did groups gain as expected, were
  risks and unintended effects foreseen (10.10).
- The evaluation plan lists its aims, types, data needed and how it will
  be collected, methods, risks, timeline with review points, scope for
  early piloting, budget, and governance with a named lead analyst
  (guidance 11.40).

**Quality of the thinking.** The guidance says a business case is
"thinking, not writing" (3.4), that it needs a collaboration of
functions, stakeholder engagement and honest statements of evidence
quality (3.23), and that workshops are recommended at the decision
points (3.24, five workshops named). It warns that a case can be
"rigged" or "retrofitted" to a predetermined solution (8.5).

**Statutory duties.** Equality, environment and biodiversity duties for
England, with a well-being duty in Wales, are listed in the guidance
(3.29, 3.30). These are law in one jurisdiction. The shape (a named duty,
considered at each decision, with the result recorded) is general. The
content is not.

**Agile and iterative work (HM Treasury and CDDO clarification).**
Discovery and alpha are treated as learning phases that feed an evolving
business case and a wider programme case. For work below a stated level
of spend, the programme business case can carry approval, with a light
outline case only for larger or riskier pieces. Review meetings use
working demonstrations, burn charts and backlogs. Funding is released in
tranches against agreed review points. The clarification applies the
principle that approval can follow learning and does not have to precede
all of it.

### 1.2 New Zealand: Better Business Cases (March 2026)

This is a UK-derived model, rebuilt. It adds structure that is worth
more to Cartograph than the UK source.

- **Two paths, then a separate decision.** A two-stage path
  (Indicative Business Case, Detailed Business Case) or one proportionate
  Single Stage Business Case (3, 2). After either, a separate
  **Approval to Deliver** decision confirms the approved option is still
  within agreed scope, cost, funding and risk before major commitments
  (2, 3.3). It "does not reopen" the strategic or economic logic.
- **Each stage says what it commits.** The Indicative case "does not
  commit funding for delivery or select a preferred option" (3.1.1).
  The Detailed case supports committing to a preferred option (3.1.2).
  Approval "authorises the commitments and funding explicitly set out in
  the decision and does not confer authority beyond what is stated"
  (1.1).
- **Authority has a boundary written down (12.1, 12.2).** The record
  must state what can start now, what stays conditional (funding
  confirmation, procurement, further governance), and what approval does
  not authorise. Staged delivery states what is authorised in the first
  stage and what must be re-confirmed. The guidance names "treating an
  Approval to Deliver as a formality" as something to avoid.
- **Escalation and return triggers (12.3).** The record lists the
  changes that would alter what decision makers thought they approved:
  cost, scope, sequencing, external changes. They must be observable by
  the delivery team. It also says who is told and what form a return
  takes (a revised paper, an updated case, a re-confirmation). It
  warns against triggers so broad that everything escalates.
- **A programme sets the rules for its projects (4).** Projects inherit
  the programme's problems, objectives, requirements, critical success
  factors, sequencing, constraints and risk position. A project's
  problems must be a logical subset. A project cannot change programme
  logic without approval, and escalation is needed where it would
  introduce a new problem, alter objectives, change scope or sequence, or
  materially affect benefits, risks or affordability. A practical test:
  would it change what programme decision makers agreed?
- **Traceable logic (1.1, 2.5).** Problem statements with cause and
  effect. Objectives from causes. Requirements from objectives, and
  these are solution-neutral. Requirements set the dimensions of choice.
  Choices are viable only if they serve an objective and fail no critical
  success factor. Effects lead to benefits for named beneficiaries. This
  is "end-to-end traceability".
- **Benefits (6.10, 10.7).** Start from effects, not aspirations. A
  benefit describes what improves, not activity. Three types: monetary,
  non-monetary, intangible, sorted by three tests (measurable,
  attributable, monetisable). Money only where credible methods and data
  exist. Weight benefits by agreed relative importance. Name beneficiaries
  and disbenefits. In management: KPIs per benefit, a **benefit profile**
  (when it starts, how it grows, when it is steady), a named **owner**
  with the handover from project team to owner made visible, and honesty
  about **contribution** where attribution is only partial.
  "Without this integration, benefits monitoring often lapses once
  delivery is complete" (10.7).
- **Design principles (1.2).** The guidance asks to be plain English and
  structured so people and automated tools can read it. Each case says
  what it is, and is not, responsible for.
- **System requirements and Māori concepts (Overview, Annex B).** A set
  of mandatory requirements from system leaders applies to every case.
  The Treasury uses He Ara Waiora to apply Māori concepts. This is the
  same shape as the UK duties, with different content.

### 1.3 World Bank operation cycle

Six stages: initiation, preparation, assessment, negotiation and
approval, implementation and supervision, completion and evaluation.
Points that bear on design:
- The borrower, not the Bank, identifies and prepares the operation. The
  design is guided by a country framework, so strategic fit is checked
  against a diagnostic and a partnership framework above the project.
- Early documents are disclosed to the public: scope, risk summary.
- Preparation includes technical, economic, social and environmental
  assessment and consultation with beneficiaries. Assessment confirms
  outcomes, beneficiaries and the monitoring tools, and checks that
  implementers have systems for finance, procurement, risk, reporting and
  monitoring and evaluation.
- Approval is by the Board on a Project Appraisal Document.
- During implementation, "changing circumstances... can sometimes
  require adjustments to the operation design... or even objectives,
  resulting in a restructuring". Progress reviews are joint and
  recurring.
- At the end the Bank does a **self-evaluation** (outcomes, challenges,
  lessons) and an independent group **validates all of them** and also
  runs cluster evaluations across similar closed operations.
- The page says that in 2026 the Bank moved to a risk-based model with
  more delegated decisions and less preparation time. Oversight is
  focused "where it matters most". This is proportionality again.

### 1.4 Canada: Policy on Results

This is not a business case. It is the framework for how a department
says what it is for and shows it. It is relevant because it starts from
something other than a project.
- A department has **Core Responsibilities**: "an enduring function or
  role" (Appendix A). Under each, **Departmental Results** (the changes
  it seeks to influence, "often outside" its immediate control) with
  **indicators** (4.3.1). Below that, a **Program Inventory** and, for
  each program, a **Performance Information Profile** with a named
  official responsible for it and for data collection (4.3.5 to 4.3.7).
- Changes to results and indicators need central approval (4.3.2). New
  or renewed spending submissions must be backed by performance and
  evaluation information, verified in writing (4.3.10, 4.3.14).
- A **rolling evaluation plan** is approved every year. It must say what
  is covered and **give a reason for each program not scheduled** for
  evaluation (4.3.15.2, 4.3.15.3). Evaluation is run by a neutral
  function with direct access to the head of the department (4.3.11,
  4.3.12). Reports and management responses are published (4.3.18).
- Resource decisions are meant to follow performance (Objectives, 3).

### 1.5 Other domains

**EU PM² (project management).** Four phases: initiating, planning,
executing, closing. Three approval gates: Ready for Planning, Ready for
Executing (v3.0 wording "Ready for Execution"), Ready for Closing
(slide 26). The business case and project charter come out of initiating
(slides 55 onward). Outputs are "merely a means to an end"; outcomes
and benefits "are often realized only after the project has closed"
(slide 11). Logs for risks, issues, decisions and changes run through
every phase. A project-end report records lessons learned.

**RIBA Plan of Work 2020 (construction).** Eight stages, from Stage 0
Strategic Definition to Stage 7 Use. Stage 0 defines the business case
and client requirements and should be strategic, with a note that a
building "might not be the most appropriate solution". The client signs
off a Stage Report at the end of each stage. Project Outcomes are set in
the brief. **Plan for Use** applies Soft Landings: set measurable
targets, do the handover and aftercare activities, evaluate performance
and feed lessons back. The 2020 edition added Stage 0 and Stage 7
because the earlier plan ended at handover.

**Scrum (agile software).** Empirical: transparency, inspection,
adaptation. The Product Goal is the long-term objective, held in the
backlog, and "the rest of the Product Backlog emerges" (Commitment:
Product Goal). Each Sprint is a short project whose goal can be
cancelled if it becomes obsolete. Inspection and adaptation toward the
Product Goal happen at least monthly. It says forecasts "do not replace"
empiricism. A product has no end date unless someone ends it, and "could
be a service". So decisions are small, frequent and reversible, and the
unit of record is a goal and a backlog, not a case.

**SAFe Lean Portfolio Management (agile at scale).** One-line definition
only: it aligns strategy and execution by applying lean thinking to
strategy and investment funding, agile portfolio operations and
governance. Funding by value stream is implied. I could not read the
detail.

**Horizon Europe (research funding).** The glossary separates outputs,
outcomes and impacts and defines a **pathway to impact**: the steps
from a project's results, through dissemination and use, to wider
impact "over time, in particular beyond the duration of a project".
Milestones are control points that can be decision points and "should
be verifiable". The programme itself monitors through nine **Key Impact
Pathways**, reported during and after the programme. Applicants are told
not to confuse outcome and impact.

**Impact Management Project, Five Dimensions of Impact (impact
investing, philanthropy).** What outcome and how important. Who
experiences it, with differences between groups. How much, in number,
degree and duration. **Contribution**: whether results were likely better
than would have happened anyway. **Risk**: the likelihood that impact
differs from what was expected.

**OKRs (corporate).** An objective is what is to be achieved. Key
results measure how you get there, and should be "measurable and
verifiable". Key results change as work progresses and an objective may
last a year or more. A short cycle of grading is typical. This is a
steering method for goals and results. It does not cover the decision to
invest.

**ISO 21500 (generic).** Abstract only: it sets the concepts for projects,
programmes and portfolios for any organisation. It says nothing I could
use about decisions.

**US OMB A-11 (federal budget).** The guide lists a performance framework
covering strategic planning, annual plans, reviews, program and project
management and evidence and evaluation. Same family as Canada's policy.
Not read further.

**IATI, OECD DAC, Charity Commission.** IATI is a publication standard
for aid activities; I read only its landing page. OECD DAC and the
charity rules I could not read. They are named so that the next reader
knows these are the places to look next, not because I drew from them.

## 2. The structure of a decision to invest, across the sources

Names differ and the shape is the same.

| Is the problem real and the direction right? | Strategic Outline Case | Indicative Business Case | Business Case | Stage 0 to 1 | Preparation, Assessment | Departmental Results | Product Backlog refined |
| Which option, with what plan? | Outline Business Case | Detailed Business Case | Planning, Ready for Executing | Stage 1 to 4 | Assessment, approval | Program Inventory | Sprint Goal each cycle |

What is deferred, by stage, in the UK and NZ sources:
- The first approval does not commit money and does not choose a final
  option (NZ 3.1.1).
- Detailed commercial, financial and management arrangements are drawn
  progressively (guidance Figures 3 to 5).
- Contracts wait for the last approval (guidance 4.24; NZ 12.2).
- Benefits are first named at the case for change, then profiled and
  owned when delivery is planned, then tracked after handover.
- Evaluation is planned before final approval and carried out later.

What the frameworks leave open: who approves in a body with no minister
(UK 3.14 lists ministers, accounting officers, an investment committee,
the Treasury, councillors), what "proportionate" means, and whether the
stages are mandatory.

## 3. Design criteria

Key. **E** essential, **D** desirable. Domains: **G** government and
public finance (UK, NZ, Canada, US), **M** multilateral and aid (World
Bank, DAC, IATI), **C** construction (RIBA), **A** agile (Scrum, SAFe),
**R** research funding (Horizon Europe), **I** impact and philanthropy
(Impact Frontiers), **P** generic project standards (PM², ISO 21500),
**B** corporate (OKRs). "Neutral" means the criterion holds in all
domains that were read. "Specific" says where it does not.

### 3.1 Staging and authority

**G1-01 (E).** A person can see which decision a record is asking for
now (for example: agree to look at it, agree the direction, commit,
confirm before an irreversible step, continue or stop), and who is asked.
- Source: UK guidance 4.8 to 4.24; NZ 3, 12.1; PM² slide 26; RIBA stage
  reports; World Bank cycle stages 1 to 4.
- Domains: G, M, C, P. Agile (A) decides continuously, see 4.1.
- Neutral. Names and counts of stages differ, the idea of named decisions
  does not.

**G1-02 (E).** Each approval records what it authorises, what it leaves
conditional, and what it does not authorise.
- Source: NZ 1.1, 12.1, 12.2; TAP 5.2 (approve, approve with
  conditions, reset); UK guidance 4.14.
- Domains: G, M (conditions on lending), C (stage sign-off).
- Neutral in idea. The explicit "does not authorise" list comes from NZ
  alone.

**G1-03 (E).** A record names the person or body who approves it, and the
person who answers for the outcome, before the detail is written.
- Source: UK guidance 6.4 and Annex B (mandate, SRO, approving authority
  signed at the start); NZ 1.1; World Bank (Board approves; borrower
  prepares); PM² (project owner and steering).
- Domains: G, M, P.
- Neutral. A cooperative or a hobbyist names a person or a meeting.
  Cartograph calls the owner a role, and that is fine.

**G1-04 (E).** The depth the record is expected to reach is agreed at the
start with the approver, and a small piece of work can take a short path.
- Source: Green Book 1.8; UK guidance 3.21, 3.22, 4.3 to 4.5, Annex B;
  NZ 2.6 and single-stage path; World Bank 2026 risk-based processing;
  agile clarification (light case below a spend level).
- Domains: G, M, A.
- Neutral. The Green Book lists proportionality as a core principle, and
  every framework that tried to scale down has done so by shortening the
  path, not by weakening the logic (NZ 2: "the same logic applies").

**G1-05 (E).** A record can say when the answer to a decision is "stop",
"hold", "postpone" or "reset", with a reason, as readily as "go".
- Source: UK guidance 4.14 (postpone or abandon); TAP 5.2, 5.17 (reset);
  Scrum (sprint cancelled when its goal is obsolete); World Bank
  (restructuring); Canada (evaluation of ongoing programs).
- Domains: G, M, A.
- Neutral. Cartograph's PortfolioDecisions (invest, hold, stop, with
  reason and date) already do this at one level.

**G1-06 (E).** After approval, a change that would alter what the
approver agreed is recognisable from the record, and it returns to the
approver. The record lists the triggers in terms the delivery team can
observe.
- Source: NZ 4.3 and 12.3; TAP 5.17; World Bank restructuring; Scrum.
- Domains: G, M, A.
- Neutral in idea. The "list the triggers in advance" form comes from NZ.
  For a hobbyist this can be one line.

**G1-07 (E).** The record is kept up to date after approval, and each
material change is logged against the version that was approved.
- Source: UK guidance 3.19, 4.25, 5.7, 5.15; TAP 5.9; World Bank
  implementation reviews; Scrum inspect and adapt.
- Domains: G, M, A, P.
- Neutral.

**G1-08 (D).** Early learning can be funded and approved as a small
decision on its own, with its aim stated as reducing named uncertainties,
before the full case is made.
- Source: agile clarification (discovery and alpha feed an evolving case);
  Scrum empiricism; UK guidance 6.6 (opportunity framing workshop);
  Green Book 6.63 (reduce uncertainty by pilots).
- Domains: G, A, R (proposals fund early work).
- Neutral. How much money counts as small is local and must not be a
  rule.

### 3.2 Rationale, objectives and fit

**G1-09 (E).** A record states the problem with its cause and its effect,
and says who is affected, before it states a solution.
- Source: NZ 1.1, 6.4 to 6.6; Green Book 4.2, 4.9; UK guidance 7.14.
  Logframe practice is from the earlier rounds.
- Domains: G, M. RIBA Stage 0 asks the same ("whether a building is the
  best means").
- Neutral.

**G1-10 (E).** Objectives are few, state a change in outcome, can be
checked, and are kept separate from outputs and from benefits.
- Source: Green Book 4.20 to 4.26; UK guidance 7.7; Horizon glossary
  (outputs, outcomes, impacts); PM² slides 11 and 12; OKRs (objective
  versus key results).
- Domains: G, R, P, B, M.
- Neutral. Impact Frontiers asks the same questions in other words (what,
  who, how much).

**G1-11 (E).** Each piece of work shows what it serves above it, and the
chain upward can be followed to the organisation's purpose.
- Source: Green Book 3.4, 3.5; UK guidance 3.12, 6.5; NZ 4.1;
  Canada (core responsibility, results, program); World Bank (country
  framework above the project); Horizon (programme destination above the
  topic); Scrum (Product Goal); OKRs (aligned objectives).
- Domains: G, M, R, A, B.
- Neutral. This is the strongest cross-domain agreement in the research.

**G1-12 (E).** What a piece of work inherits from the level above is
recorded, and a change that would alter higher-level objectives or scope
is routed to the higher-level owner.
- Source: NZ 4.1 to 4.3; Green Book 3.6 (project need set by programme);
  UK guidance 5.3.
- Domains: G. Canada has the same rule for results frameworks (central
  approval to change them, 4.3.2).
- Neutral in idea, formalised in G only.

**G1-13 (E).** The baseline, meaning what happens if nothing new is done,
is described with its own costs, benefits and risks, and is the reference
for options and for measures.
- Source: Green Book 4.14 to 4.19; UK guidance 7.10 to 7.12; Impact
  Frontiers contribution (better than what would have happened
  otherwise); NZ 6.6 (effects if nothing changes); PM² and PRINCE2 (do
  nothing, from earlier research).
- Domains: G, I, P.
- Neutral.

**G1-14 (D).** Requirements, constraints and critical success factors are
recorded as conditions any option must meet, written without a solution.
- Source: NZ 6.9, 6.12, 2.5; Green Book 5.5, 5.6; UK guidance 7.27.
- Domains: G. RIBA's client requirements play the same part.
- Mostly G and C. In a small organisation, a short list of "must be true"
  does the same work.

### 3.3 Options

**G1-15 (E, for any decision with a cost or consequence the approver
would want to weigh; D otherwise).** A record can hold the options that
were considered, including doing nothing and the minimum that meets the
objective, and the reason each was taken forward or dropped.
- Source: Green Book 5.1 to 5.4, 5.18, 2.16; UK guidance 8.5 (guards
  against a "rigged" case); NZ 7; World Bank Project Paper (alternative
  scenarios); RIBA Stage 0.
- Domains: G, M, C. Scrum treats alternatives as backlog experiments.
- Neutral in principle. Strong in G, M, C, and lighter elsewhere.

**G1-16 (D).** Options can be built from choices along named dimensions
(for example scope, solution, who delivers, how it is phased, how it is
paid for), each choice marked as best, workable or rejected with a reason.
- Source: Green Book 5.21 to 5.35; NZ 7.5 to 7.8 (dimensions of choice
  derived from requirements).
- Domains: G only. The Green Book itself makes it recommended and "not
  mandatory" (5.13).
- Specific to public investment. Offer as an optional method, not as the
  structure of the record.

**G1-17 (E).** Options are compared on the same attributes side by side,
and no single score or ratio decides.
- Source: Green Book 6.96 to 6.101, 9.3 to 9.6 (appraisal summary table);
  Green Book 5.15 (against simple weighting and scoring).
- Domains: G. NZ differs, see 4.3.
- Neutral in idea.

**G1-18 (D).** Value for money is judged on several named factors
(performance on objectives, money, things that cannot be priced, who
gains and loses, risk), and the record shows each.
- Source: Green Book Box 1 (2.6); NZ 7.16 (public value summary).
- Domains: G. Impact Frontiers is a parallel for I.
- Neutral in the factors. The label "value for money" is G.

### 3.4 Benefits and their keeping

**G1-19 (E).** Each intended benefit names who gains, how it is measured,
the starting value, who answers for it, and when it is expected to
appear.
- Source: UK guidance 7.24, 11.21; NZ 6.10, 10.7 (profile, owner);
  PM² slide 11 (benefits after closing); Impact Frontiers (who, how much,
  how long).
- Domains: G, P, I.
- Neutral.

**G1-20 (E).** A person can record a disbenefit: someone who loses, or a
cost to people that is not a cost to the organisation.
- Source: NZ 6.10; Green Book 6.15, 6.23 (who bears costs); Impact
  Frontiers (outcomes positive or negative; who).
- Domains: G, I.
- Neutral.

**G1-21 (E).** A benefit can be recorded as measurable, as only
describable, or as unknown, without forcing a money value.
- Source: NZ 6.10 (measurable, attributable, monetisable tests; money only
  where methods and data exist); Green Book 6.26 to 6.28 (monetise,
  quantify, describe).
- Domains: G. Others (I, R) never monetise by default.
- Neutral. The Green Book prefers money where credible. Cartograph should
  not require it.

**G1-22 (E).** The record says how far the organisation's own work caused
a result, and does not claim all of a change it only contributed to.
- Source: NZ 10.7; Impact Frontiers (contribution); Canada Appendix A
  (results "often outside" a department's control); Horizon (pathway
  beyond the project).
- Domains: G, I, R.
- Neutral.

**G1-23 (E).** When a project ends, someone is named to keep watching its
benefits, and the readings carry on under that name.
- Source: NZ 10.7; UK guidance 4.25, Table 2 Gate 5; PM² slide 11; RIBA
  Stage 7 and Plan for Use; Canada (program official responsible for the
  profile).
- Domains: G, C, P. Scrum has no project end.
- Neutral. Cartograph's ongoing services are the natural home.

**G1-24 (D).** Benefits can be given relative weights agreed by the
approver.
- Source: NZ 6.10 only. Contrast Green Book 5.15.
- Domains: G (NZ).
- Specific to NZ. Keep optional.

### 3.5 Uncertainty and risk

**G1-25 (E).** Assumptions the case depends on are written, with how
confident the author is, and what would show them wrong. They are kept
apart from risks.
- Source: Green Book 2.10, 4.12, 6.61, 6.63; UK guidance 7.21 to 7.29
  (risks, constraints, dependencies as separate lists); Horizon (pathway
  conditions); Impact Frontiers risk.
- Domains: G, R, I.
- Neutral. Cartograph's Assumption kind already holds this.

**G1-26 (D).** A forecast of cost, time or benefit can be given as a
central value with a range, and the record can show how far a key
assumption could move before the choice would change.
- Source: Green Book 6.68 to 6.72, 6.102 to 6.105; optimism bias guidance
  3.18, 4.1.
- Domains: G, M (economic analysis). Impact Frontiers has "risk" as a
  dimension.
- Specific to appraisal-led domains. The idea of a "what would change my
  mind" value is neutral. The UK percentages are not.

**G1-27 (D).** The organisation can compare what it forecast with what
happened, to learn how optimistic it tends to be, and any adjustment is
tied to the organisation's own record and to verified evidence.
- Source: Green Book 6.79, 6.80, 10.5; optimism bias guidance 1.2, 3.17,
  3.19.
- Domains: G. A similar case is in PM² lessons learned and World Bank
  completion reports.
- Neutral in idea. Do not ship generic percentages.

**G1-28 (E).** Each risk has an owner, and where public money or grants
are paid out, fraud and error are considered.
- Source: UK guidance 11.28, 11.31; Green Book 2.24, 6.64.
- Domains: G, M (fiduciary controls in the World Bank cycle).
- Owner: neutral. Fraud and error: specific to money paid to others.

### 3.6 Money and procurement

**G1-29 (E).** Whether the work is worth doing, and whether it can be
afforded from named sources, are two different answers and are kept
apart in the record.
- Source: Green Book Table 9; NZ Annex A (affordability "does not assess
  public value"); guidance chapters 8 and 10 (economic case versus
  financial case).
- Domains: G. Philanthropy and corporate budgets make the same split in
  practice, but I did not read a source.
- Neutral in idea. Cartograph's FundingSource and Allocation fit the
  second half.

**G1-30 (D).** Where goods or services are bought, the record holds the
route, the split of risk between buyer and supplier, and the payment
basis.
- Source: UK guidance chapter 9 (procurement strategy, risk allocation,
  payment mechanisms, contracts); NZ 8.
- Domains: G, M (fiduciary and procurement plan in the World Bank cycle),
  C.
- Specific to organisations that procure. A cooperative with volunteers
  does not need it.

### 3.7 Review, evaluation and learning

**G1-31 (E).** Someone other than the author can review a record at a
decision point, and the record shows that they did and what they
concluded.
- Source: UK guidance 3.26, 4.6, 4.7; TAP chapter 3 (not read in full);
  World Bank (independent group validates all self-evaluations); Canada
  4.3.11 (neutral evaluation function); PM² gates.
- Domains: G, M, P. Scrum relies on the team and stakeholders in the
  Review.
- Neutral in idea. The formal gate structure is G. Cartograph's change
  sets and review pages already give a person a way to review.

**G1-32 (E).** An evaluation or review is planned when the case is made,
says what question it will answer, what data it needs and who leads it.
It also says why some work is not to be evaluated.
- Source: Green Book 10.7 to 10.9; UK guidance 11.40; TAP 2.14; Canada
  4.3.15.2 and 4.3.15.3 (reason for programs not evaluated).
- Domains: G, M (completion report). Impact Frontiers and Horizon expect
  impact reporting.
- Neutral. The Canada rule is a good check against both too much and too
  little evaluation.

**G1-33 (E).** What is measured, how often, and who is told, is agreed in
advance, and the reasons are both learning and answerability.
- Source: UK guidance 11.34 to 11.37; Green Book 10.3; Canada (performance
  information profile with responsible official and data collection);
  Scrum (inspection cadence); World Bank (recurring joint reviews).
- Domains: G, M, A.
- Neutral. Cartograph's KPI reporting cycle covers part of this.

**G1-34 (D).** On completion, the record holds a short account of what
was achieved against what was approved, what went well and badly, and
lessons for the next decision.
- Source: World Bank stage 6; PM² Project-End Report; RIBA Plan for Use
  (feed back lessons); Green Book 10.10; Canada (management responses).
- Domains: M, P, C, G.
- Neutral.

**G1-35 (D).** The owner can choose to make a decision and its evaluation
public.
- Source: TAP 5.13 to 5.15; Green Book 10.11, 10.12; Canada 4.3.18;
  World Bank (early documents disclosed); IATI (publication standard,
  landing page only).
- Domains: G, M. Not found in R, I, A in what I read.
- Specific in strength. Only G and M require it. Neutral as an option.

### 3.8 People, law and the shape of the record

**G1-36 (E).** The record shows who took part in preparing each decision
(including the people affected) and what they said, without requiring a
workshop.
- Source: UK guidance 3.23, 3.24 (stakeholder engagement and workshops);
  Green Book 5.22; NZ Overview (He Ara Waiora); World Bank preparation
  (beneficiaries consulted); Impact Frontiers (who).
- Domains: G, M, I.
- Neutral for the record of consultation. The formal facilitated
  workshop is G.

**G1-37 (D).** An organisation can list the laws, funder conditions and
internal policies it must show it considered, and attach to a decision how
each was addressed. The list is the organisation's own.
- Source: Green Book 3.18; UK guidance 3.29, 3.30; NZ Annex B; Canada
  (policy suite).
- Domains: G in every country read. The funder-conditions form is common
  in M and R but I did not read a source for it.
- Shape neutral, content jurisdictional. This is the one place where UK
  names (Equality Act, EPPS) must not appear in a shared contract.

**G1-38 (E).** The decision paper is short and stands alone. The record
behind it holds the detail and can be reached from it.
- Source: UK guidance 3.4 ("thinking, not writing"), 3.18 (executive
  summary); NZ 5 (executive summary, one to two pages, as a guide); PM²
  (business case, charter); Cartograph D55 (charter is a brief).
- Domains: G, P.
- Neutral.

**G1-39 (E).** One record serves several views (why, choice, money,
delivery, benefits) and a change in one is checked against the others.
It is not five forms.
- Source: Green Book 3.15, 3.16; UK guidance 2.3; NZ 2.5.
- Domains: G, M (the same ideas under other names).
- Neutral. The five case names are G.

### 3.9 Things that are not services of a project

**G1-40 (E).** A continuing service or function is a subject of the same
decisions as a project: start it, change it, review it, stop it. It has
results, indicators, an owner and an evaluation plan of its own.
- Source: Canada (Core Responsibility, enduring function; program
  inventory; five-year evaluation requirement for ongoing grants); RIBA
  Stage 7 Use; Scrum (product has no end date, may be a service); UK
  guidance Gate 5 (service life); World Bank sustaining benefits.
- Domains: G, C, A, M.
- Neutral, and the UK project-centred sources are the odd ones out.

## 4. Where the frameworks disagree or leave room

### 4.1 Fixed stages or a living record with decision points

- **Fixed stages** (UK three stages; NZ two plus Approval to Deliver; PM²
  three gates; RIBA eight stages; World Bank six). Good: everyone knows
  what is decided when. Bad: heavy for small work, and people skip stages
  or fake them.
- **Continuous** (Scrum; Canada's rolling plans; the UK agile
  clarification). Good: matches how learning happens. Bad: no obvious
  moment when someone agrees to commit.
- Every fixed-stage framework already allows both: UK combined stages and
  a one-stage justification case, NZ's single-stage path.
- **Recommendation.** One living record, with a small, named set of
  decision points the owner chooses at the start (G1-04). The default
  path is short. The record, not the stage, is the unit.

### 4.2 Money values

- UK: monetise "where possible" (Green Book 6.28). NZ: only where
  credible and proportionate, and say so if not (6.10). Impact Frontiers
  and Horizon do not use money. Canada uses results indicators.
- **Recommendation.** Record the class (priced, counted, described,
  unknown). Never require money. Leave discounting and valuation to
  specialists and tools outside Cartograph. UK rates must not be built in.

### 4.3 Weighting and scoring

- Green Book: against simple weighted scoring (5.15), for expert-led
  multi-criteria decision analysis (5.14), and for a side-by-side table
  (9.3). NZ: weights agreed by the approver, summing to a whole (6.10).
- **Recommendation.** Side-by-side by default. Allow weights only as
  stated priorities, and show them. Do not produce one total.

### 4.4 Problem first or objective first

- NZ and logframes: problem, then objective. UK guidance 7.9: objectives
  before existing arrangements, to avoid defining the aim in terms of
  current systems.
- Both agree that the solution comes last. Cartograph's walk already puts
  Problem before Objectives.
- **Recommendation.** Keep problem first. Ask for the baseline (G1-13)
  after the objective.

### 4.5 Is a benefit its own record?

- Sources: UK and NZ describe a benefits register with owner, baseline,
  timing and category. Cartograph's D4 decided against a Benefit kind
  because the parts are held by Goal, KeyResult, KPI, BeneficiaryGroup
  and success criteria.
- Check against the sources. Owner, baseline and beneficiary are held.
  Category, timing profile and disbenefit are not (I looked at the KPI
  and goal schemas and found no field for either).
- Options: (a) a new kind, which D4 rejects for duplication; (b) fields
  on the existing parts plus a **register view** that reads across them;
  (c) leave as it is.
- **Recommendation.** (b). D4's reasoning holds. The sources ask for the
  register and its columns, not for a new noun.

### 4.6 How independent review must be

- UK: gate reviews, with a central body. World Bank: an independent group.
  Canada: a neutral function with direct access to the head. Scrum: the
  team itself.
- A hobbyist cannot hire a reviewer.
- **Recommendation.** A record can name a reviewer who did not write it
  (G1-31). Whether that is a gate team or a friend is the owner's choice.
  Show when the record has had no outside look.

### 4.7 Whole-case approval or staged authority

- UK approves a case at a stage. NZ adds a separate Approval to Deliver
  and staged authority within delivery. The agile clarification releases
  money in tranches against review points.
- **Recommendation.** Approval attaches to a version and states what it
  covers (G1-02). Tranches are conditions on that approval.

### 4.8 Statutory duties

- UK, Wales and NZ each hang their own list on the process.
- **Recommendation.** An organisation-defined list (G1-37). Do not put
  jurisdiction-specific names in the shared contract.

## 5. Cartograph today, set against these sources

Read: `TAXONOMY.md` (definitions, mapping, D4, D6, D12, D24, D30, D32,
D33, D52, D54 to D57), the project flow, the project, programme,
operation, portfolio, goal, gap, KPI and assumption schemas, ADR 0022
(change sets), the guide's explanation page on project charters, and the
interface's `copy.ts` for Home and the route list. I did not read the
whole of `copy.ts` or run the interface.

### 5.1 What is strong

- **The golden thread is built in.** Purpose, goals, objectives, outcomes,
  gaps, programmes, projects and operations are linked, and the Strategy
  view reads top-down. This is G1-11 and is the best-supported feature.
- **Continuing services are first-class.** Operations, with planned,
  running and retired status (D30), meet G1-40. The UK sources are the
  ones that miss it. Canada, RIBA and Scrum support it.
- **Portfolio decisions record go, hold and stop with reason and date**
  (D32). This meets G1-05 for portfolios.
- **Programmes are held to a theory of change** (D32, `pathway`), and
  assumptions have their own kind (`Assumption`), as G1-25 asks. The
  guide's own text says the assumption kind was added because risk lists
  never used the assumption type.
- **Sign-offs and conditions are events on the record** (D52), which fits
  G1-02 and part of G1-07.
- **Governance and the mandate come before scope and plan** (D33). That
  matches the UK scoping document, which fixes the mandate, SRO and
  approver first (6.4, Annex B).
- **Change sets give review.** Agents and people work in a change set that
  is reviewed before it is merged (ADR 0022). This is a base for G1-31.
- **The charter is a brief** (D55), as G1-38 asks.
- **Honest unknowns.** A KPI baseline must be a value or an admitted
  unknown with a date (KPI `baseline`).

### 5.2 What the sources show is missing

1. **There is no record of the choice.** There is no place for options,
   for business as usual, or for why the chosen course beat the others
   (G1-13, G1-15). `TAXONOMY.md` maps "business case" to
   `Project.spec.funding` and `mandate`, and calls that "partial and
   deliberate". Against NZ and the Green Book, that is the largest
   structural gap. It is also the one the guide already names. Cartograph
   describes itself as a tool that captures plans. A captured plan holds
   the answer. It does not hold the decision, and the sources are about
   the decision.
2. **Decisions are not a thing.** Sign-offs by stage and conditions exist
   for a project. Only portfolios have go, hold, stop. There is nowhere
   to record "this programme was approved to the next stage on this
   date, covering this", or "this service was reviewed and continued"
   (G1-01, G1-02, G1-05 at levels below portfolio).
3. **No trigger list.** `escalationRoute` says where a matter goes. A
   KPI `response` says what is done when a reading signals trouble. Nothing
   says what changes in cost, scope or timing send the decision back
   (G1-06).
4. **No evaluation or learning.** I found no evaluation plan, lessons or
   completion review in the schemas (a text search found only a mention of
   "benefits realised"). G1-32 and G1-34 are not met. The Canada rule
   (say why something is not evaluated) has no home.
5. **Benefits lack category, timing and disbenefits** (G1-19, G1-20, G1-21,
   4.5 above). Owner and baseline are present.
6. **No contribution statement** (G1-22).
7. **No uncertainty on forecasts** (G1-26). Risks exist. Ranges and
   "how wrong can this be" do not.
8. **No forecast-against-outturn history** (G1-27). The events log (D52)
   could feed it, but nothing reads across it.
9. **No agreement on depth** (G1-04). The walk is the same for everyone.
   Walk-first and the "full workspace one step away" decision address
   screen load, not the question "how much should this record hold?".
10. **No statement of who was consulted** (G1-36) beyond a stakeholder map.
11. **No place for the organisation's own list of rules it must satisfy**
    (G1-37), other than the deprecated `compliance` field.

### 5.3 What the sources make look irrelevant or misplaced

I try to be candid and not to condemn.

- **Most of the project walk's later steps are delivery, not decision.**
  Schedule, costs, data, risks and procurement detail belong in the
  UK sources to the outline business case at most, and to delivery plans
  otherwise (the Teal Book, in G3's scope). The guide already splits "later
  planning" from the charter. G1 adds that the *decision to invest* is
  made on the strategic and economic parts and the management summary.
  Cartograph asks for plan detail from the start of every project, so the
  decision-relevant half comes in the same walk as the delivery half.
  Splitting along the sources' line (decide, then plan) matches the
  walk-first finding.
- **The dbt semantic layer for KPIs (D57, D63 to D65)** is not supported
  by any decision framework read. What they do support is what it serves:
  the data exists, there is a baseline, a named reporter (NZ 10.7;
  Canada's Performance Information Profile). The metric algebra is an
  adapter for data teams. It should stay optional and out of the way, as
  the engine already says ("a team with no warehouse never sees the step").
- **"Project" as the centre.** The Green Book is organised around
  proposals at four levels. NZ and Canada show that the decision
  structure applies equally to a programme, a service and a portfolio.
  Cartograph puts the walk's centre and the charter at the project. The
  sources suggest the centre is the **decision**, at any level, over a
  living record. This is a judgement on my part, and G5 judges the
  current product directly.
- **Names.** "Business case", "strategic outline case" and "Approval to
  Deliver" are government terms. Impact Frontiers, Scrum and RIBA say the
  same things in other words. The shared contract should use plain,
  neutral names, and the UI should use the user's own vocabulary where it
  can (D71 already lets an organisation set its own choices).
- **Fixed lists from one jurisdiction** (statutory duties, discount rates,
  time horizons, optimism bias tables, BCR thresholds). None should enter
  a shared contract. All could be an organisation's own list or an
  adapter.

### 5.4 Diátaxis lens

- **Tutorial (learning).** NZ's emphasis on plain English and a thin
  executive summary fits the onboarding walk people liked.
- **How-to (doing a task).** "Make a decision for a piece of work" is a
  task with a clear start (the question, G1-09) and end (an approval
  with its limits, G1-02). The sources show it as a sequence of questions,
  which is what a walk is.
- **Reference (looking up).** The register views: benefits, assumptions,
  decisions, triggers. A person checking "what did we approve and what is
  left conditional" needs these, and they are not a walk.
- **Explanation (understanding).** The sources spend most of their words
  here (why options, why business as usual, why not a ratio). This
  belongs in the glossary and guide, and the UI should keep it out of the
  way.

## 6. A shortlist, if the rebuild keeps one idea from this report

Drawn from the criteria above, ordered by how many domains support them.

1. A **decision** is a recorded thing at every level, attached to a
   version, with what it covers and what it does not (G1-01, 02, 05, 06).
2. The **chain upward** and **inheritance** from the level above (G1-11,
   12).
3. **Business as usual and options** are held, with reasons (G1-13, 15).
4. A **benefit register view** over existing parts, with owner, timing,
   beneficiary and disbenefit (G1-19, 20, 23).
5. **Evaluation planned at approval**, and a short account at the end
   (G1-32, 34).
6. **Proportion agreed at the start** (G1-04), so the same record serves a
   hobbyist and a ministry.

## 7. Open questions

- I could not read the OECD DAC criteria, the Charity Commission rules,
  NHS England's own guidance, IRIS+, the Kellogg guide, GAO's guides, or
  US OMB A-11 Part 6. Criteria that touch evaluation (G1-32, G1-34) and
  impact (G1-22) would be firmer with them.
- ISO 21500 and 21502 are paywalled. I have only the abstract.
- I did not read Green Book chapter 8 (valuation), the wellbeing,
  discounting and place-based supplements, or Treasury Approvals Process
  chapters 3 and 4, which are about central assurance and scrutiny.
- I read no source from the corporate balanced scorecard literature, and
  OKRs only at a vendor site, so the corporate column is thin.
- No source I read covers a decision made by a group with no hierarchy
  (a cooperative voting, a community). The idea of an approver may need
  a "decided by a meeting on this date" form. This is a design question
  for the owner.
- Whether the "decision" should be a new kind in the contract or an event
  on existing kinds (like sign-offs, D52). This needs the engine
  architecture documents, which G5 covers.
- I did not test any of this with users. Whether people will answer
  "what else could you do?" in a walk is untested. The sources show
  organisations skip it, which the Green Book says outright (5.3).
