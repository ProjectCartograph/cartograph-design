start, and what does that ask of a tool that helps an organisation define

## 1. What the UK sources say

### 1.1 Evaluation is part of the design, not a stage after delivery

- The Magenta Book says evaluation should be built into an intervention's
  design and delivery from the earliest stages (page description and
  Executive summary). It uses "intervention" to mean any policy,
  programme, project or regulation (key terminology table), so what it
  says applies to a project as much as to a policy.
- It gives two purposes: **learning** and **accountability** (1.3, and
  Green Book 10.3 uses the same two). Learning means managing risk and
  uncertainty and making better decisions; it says plainly that
  "learning is about good decision-making" (1.3.1). Accountability means
  showing the money was well spent (1.3.2).
- Section 1.6 gives three reasons to plan early: the evaluation delivers
  findings that those designing and running the thing can use; it is of
  the right quality; and the right data are collected in the cheapest
  way. It says small design changes can make the difference between a
  useful evaluation and one that cannot answer the key questions, and
  that baseline data and a comparison group may have to be arranged
  before delivery starts.
- Section 1.5 sets out what evaluation asks **before** (how is it
  expected to work, why might it not, where are the risks and
  uncertainties, what baseline will change be measured against),
  **during** (is it delivered as intended, early signs, unintended
  consequences, can it be adjusted in flight) and **after** (did it work,
  by how much, at what cost, what did we learn).
- The ROAMEF cycle (1.4): Rationale, Objectives, Appraisal, Monitoring,
  Evaluation, Feedback. The Magenta Book stresses that it is a simple
  picture of a messy reality: the steps inform each other and rarely run
  in a line. The **Feedback** step ("what have we learnt, how will we use
  these results in future") is the one tools usually drop.
- Green Book 1.10 and 10.1: appraisal comes before approval; monitoring
  and evaluation come after. Practitioners must "consider from the outset
  how the proposal will be evaluated and what resources will be
  required". 10.7: develop an evaluation plan as soon as there is a
  preferred option, and have a robust plan in place before final
  approval. The plan draws heavily on the theory of change. 10.8: the
  costs of monitoring and evaluation go in the economic and financial
  parts of the business case. 10.9: no fixed share of the budget; it is
  case by case and proportionate, and new and untested approaches need
  more.
- **What is asked, and when** (Magenta Book 1.7, Figure 1.3, which maps
  to the three business case stages):
  - Strategic stage: a decision on the scale of a proportionate
    evaluation, the likely scale of data needs, an initial estimate of
    budget and resources, and a discussion of scope for piloting.
  - Outline stage: main objectives of monitoring and evaluation, likely
    uses and users, a high-level timetable against the policy timetable,
    and an outline budget.
  - Full stage: who is responsible for data access with delivery
    partners, an initial set of evaluation questions, the research
    likely required, and the budget.
  The lesson for a tool is that **what is asked of the person grows as
  the work firms up**, and the first ask is small: how much checking
  later is proportionate, and roughly what data it will need.
- Monitoring and evaluation plans sit in the **management case** of the
  five-case business case (1.7; Green Book Table 2 on the five cases).
  Appraisal and evaluation plans "should reflect each other", so that the
  costs and benefits appraised can later be measured (1.7).

### 1.2 The theory of change comes first

- Magenta 2.2.1: "Good policymaking necessitates a thorough
  understanding of the intervention" and the theory of change is the way
  to get it. It should be the first step in any evaluation (chapter 2
  summary). It captures: the problem, the change aimed at, the causal
  chain, the main actors, who is expected to be affected, the conditions
  needed for success, the **assumptions** and the strength of evidence
  behind them, and the wider context.
- It draws on **existing evidence** first (evidence synthesis). That
  reduces the evaluation and focuses it on what is uncertain (2.2.1,
  box on review and synthesis).
- It is best built with designers, implementers and beneficiaries
  together, and at minimum stress-tested with the key stakeholders. A
  large intervention may need a series of models. It "should continue to
  be developed over the lifetime of the evaluation" as evidence arrives.
- Diagrams (logic model, outcomes map, systems map) are pictures of the
  theory, not the theory (footnote 19). The sophisticated form records
  the precise mechanisms, alternative routes, assumptions, evidence and
  contextual factors, and may include a **negative programme theory**:
  the reasons the steps might not happen (2.2.1).
- Green Book 4.9 to 4.13 says the same for appraisal: a theory of change
  is the logical chain of cause and effect, it must not simply assert
  that inputs lead to outcomes, it is uncertain, and the assumptions must
  be stated so that decision makers agree them. 4.14 to 4.18 add the
  **business as usual** case: what is expected if nothing new is done.
  4.21: objectives must be SMART "so that they can be robustly monitored
  and evaluated". 4.25: prefer outcome objectives to output ones.
- Test and Learn annex, chapter 5: a theory of change surfaces the key
  assumptions; assumptions are sorted by **criticality** (how much they
  matter to the outcome) and **evidential strength** (how well supported
  they are). Strong evidence and high criticality: accept. Weak evidence
  and high criticality: prioritise for testing. Low criticality: monitor
  or validate (Figure 5.1). It names families of assumption: beneficiary
  relevance, expected effect, economic viability, delivery feasibility,
  potential to scale, unintended consequences.

### 1.3 Questions, types of evaluation and decision points

- Magenta 2.2.2: be clear from the start what questions the evaluation
  will answer, how the findings will be used, by whom and when. Questions
  come from the stated purpose, from the uncertainties and weak evidence
  the theory of change shows, from stakeholders, and from **decision
  points** (continue roll-out, modify, review points).
- It advises a top-down short list of high-level questions with the
  detailed ones underneath, because few evaluations can answer every
  question. Questions "typically evolve" as the work proceeds, so links
  to users must be kept (2.2.3).
- Table 2.1 lists uses of evaluation: finding risks and dependencies,
  benefits management and accountability, informing decision points,
  external scrutiny, communicating impact, understanding context,
  stakeholder engagement, and future policy.
- **Three types** (1.8, 2.2.3, Table 2.2). **Process**: what can be
  learned from how it was delivered (as intended, reached everyone
  intended, what could improve). **Impact**: what difference it made
  (outcomes, how much is attributable, for which groups, whether it can
  be repeated). **Value for money**: whether it was a good use of
  resources (costs, benefits, cost-effectiveness, compared with
  alternatives and with doing nothing). For a full picture all three are
  needed; each alone has weaknesses, for example impact findings rarely
  explain why.
- Green Book 10.4 repeats the three types and says the results feed back
  into the first stage of the next appraisal.

### 1.4 Proportionality

- Magenta 1.9: a good evaluation is **useful, credible, robust and
  proportionate**. Proportionality is "a key concept". A low-risk,
  well-evidenced, low-priority intervention needs only light-touch
  monitoring and evaluation to check it was delivered as intended and
  hit the predicted outcomes. Criteria for a priority intervention that
  needs a substantial evaluation: high profile, high uncertainty or risk,
  high cost, high learning potential (a low-priority thing can still fill
  an important evidence gap).
- Pre-registration is also "applied in a proportionate way": a short
  study registration for small evaluations, a full protocol for large
  ones (2.3.3). Transparency practices scale the same way (1.13).
- The Handling Complexity guide adds that a proportionate evaluation
  gives findings fit for purpose "given the risks", and that complex
  designs cost more because they need dialogue and flexibility (3.4).
- Green Book 1.8: use guidance proportionately. 10.9: evaluation
  funding case by case.

### 1.5 Data planned before delivery

- Magenta 4.1: data collection "needs to be planned in advance" and built
  into the policy's design and any legislation. **Baseline** data (before
  the intervention) must be collected early. **Comparison** data from
  unaffected groups must be negotiated. Without this the evaluation may
  be impossible, limited or costly. The theory of change identifies data
  needs and gaps. Using several methods builds confidence (triangulation).
- Table 4.1 gives the questions to ask: what type of data answers each
  evaluation question, who or what can provide it, are there access,
  ethical or burden problems, who collects it, from whom (census, sample,
  purposive), and how the analysis method shapes the data needed.
- 4.3.1 and Figure 4.1 give the steps for a monitoring system, "designed
  before activity starts on the ground". (1) Pick key indicators that
  can be clearly defined to track progress against the theory of change;
  targets and target dates can be set but "are not essential". (2)
  Identify the data to measure inputs, outputs, outcomes and impact. (3)
  Identify data for a comparison group if one is needed. (4) Check what
  already exists. Then: what new data fills the gaps, who is responsible
  (and do they have time and skills), when it is gathered (aligned with
  the funder's review cycle), how it is gathered and stored (formats,
  data protection, ethics, where), and how it is verified and by whom.
  It adds that the quality of monitoring data depends on the value the
  collectors see in it: if it is only an admin burden, quality is low;
  if the people who deliver use it, quality is strong.
- 4.4 data quality: assess existing data at the outset; some questions
  (sensitive characteristics) are better asked in research than in
  monitoring, while exact dates are better from administrative records.
  4.5 data handling follows data-protection law.
- 5.4 lists ways the intervention can **make the evaluation cheaper**:
  put evaluation questions into monitoring forms, collect contact details
  and consent at the start, survey all participants, pilot, and arrange
  phased roll-out or random allocation to make a comparison group. It
  notes some data collection needs legislation.

### 1.6 Counterfactuals and "what would have happened anyway"

- Magenta 2.2.4: impact evaluation must show change "over and above what
  would have happened" without the intervention. Figure 2.3 lists the
  other influences (trends, coincidence, other interventions, selection).
  The chapter gives a decision tree for approach: experimental, quasi-
  experimental, theory-based, realist, or a combination. Experimental
  approaches suit discrete, stable, short-term interventions with large
  effects; theory-based approaches suit complex, long-term, context-
  dependent ones (Figure 2.4 and Table 2.3).
- 1.7.1: benefits management (defining, tracking, realising benefits) is
  complementary to evaluation. Benefits management tracks performance
  measures; evaluation adds wider and unintended effects, attribution,
  and additionality, and can test whether assumed medium-term impacts
  follow from observed outputs.
- The Green Book's equivalent of the counterfactual for appraisal is the
  business as usual case (4.14 to 4.18) and, in evaluation, 10.10:
  review the original appraisal to see whether the theory of change
  materialised and whether costs and benefits came out as expected, for
  which groups, and whether risks were managed.
- The realist guide asks "what works, for whom, in what respects, to what
  extent, in what contexts, and how", and structures theory as context
  plus mechanism gives outcome. It lists when it is not appropriate,
  including when the average net effect is the only answer needed.

### 1.7 Governance, registration and change

- 5.3: set up governance at the start. A programme or project board
  owns the evaluation (buy-in from decision makers is "essential"); an
  evaluation steering group (evaluators, commissioners, the policy team,
  ideally delivery and participants) advises on design, methods and
  quality, and should exist before design; expert peer review at design
  and reporting.
- 2.3.3: all planned, live and completed UK central government
  evaluations from April 2024 must be **registered** (UK-specific
  requirement). Evaluation plans should be completed before the
  evaluation begins, **time-stamped** and kept. Publishing the plan
  (**pre-registration**) records what was intended so it can be compared
  with what was done. Changes are allowed but must be "documented and
  explained". The purpose is to stop adjusting analysis to find a
  favourable result.
- 5.8: balance flexibility and consistency. Decision makers want to tweak
  in flight; experimental designs need stability; flexibility is built in
  early; "a clear audit trail of changes and decisions must be
  maintained".
- 2.3.1: for a group of projects, agree **common measures** across all of
  them as far as possible, use the same instruments, give clear
  instructions to projects about what to collect, and check compliance.
  It distinguishes a delegated programme (interest in the net impact of
  the whole) from an innovation-fund programme (interest in each project
  and in what encouraged innovation).
- 2.3.2: place-based interventions need a place-level theory of change
  and attention to spillovers, displacement, and communities taking part.

### 1.8 Use and feedback

- 6.2: a use and dissemination plan answers four questions. **Which
  groups** (list them one by one); **what information** each needs;
  **which point in time** (decision points; evidence is needed before the
  final decision); **for what purpose** (a small decision may need only
  monitoring data; a large one needs more robust evidence). It can state
  **influence objectives**: what the evaluation should change. The plan
  says what is published, when, and with which channel, including the
  data and research tools.
- 6.3: a final report is "rarely the best way to ensure impact": give
  emerging findings to boards; use one-page summaries and other formats;
  agree messages with steering groups before publishing, especially for
  negative findings.
- 6.4: build an **evaluation culture**: evaluation as part of "the way we
  do things", expected at design stage, not a ritual.
- 6.5 and 1.13: the default is to publish; the level of openness is
  proportionate to cost, profile and learning value; legal and data
  sensitivity can limit it. (GSR publication principles are UK-specific.)
- Green Book 10.5: appraisals should use past evaluation evidence, and in
  particular past forecast errors to set optimism-bias adjustments. 10.11
  points to the UK evaluation registry as a source of prior evidence.

### 1.9 Complexity, test and learn

- Handling Complexity (3.6, Table 3): commissioners should ask whether
  the policy shows features of complexity, whether context variation has
  been considered, whether questions can be **revisited** as things
  change, whether data methods can be reconsidered, whether stakeholders
  accept the limits of certainty, and whether differing views in the
  steering group have been surfaced.
- Test and Learn annex (1, 2, 6): not a type of evaluation but a way of
  working: Explore, Co-design, Test, Grow. It tests the **riskiest
  assumptions** first with small, proportionate methods, adapts, and only
  then commissions a robust evaluation of the whole. It is less suited to
  well-understood problems with fixed requirements. It says governance
  should be adapted so decisions rest on evidence generated through the
  cycle rather than fixed plans (6.2).

## 2. What other domains and countries say

I give each source's own words for its own domain, then in section 3 sort
them by what they mean for the design. Section numbers are given where I
read the source at section level; otherwise the page or document.

### 2.1 OECD DAC evaluation criteria (international development; used well beyond)

Read in full. The 2019 revision keeps five criteria and adds **coherence**
(paras 1 to 9). Each is "a different lens" on an intervention (para 10).
Together they describe the attributes of a good intervention (para 11).
They are used to support accountability and learning (para 12), and also
"beyond evaluation – for monitoring and results management, and for
strategic planning and intervention design" (para 13). They can look at
process as well as results and be used before, during or after (para 14).

Definitions (paraphrased):

| Criterion | The question | Notes that matter here |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Is it doing the right things? | Objectives **and design** respond to beneficiaries', country and institution needs and priorities, and continue to do so if circumstances change. Assessing it means weighing trade-offs between priorities and looking at changes in context. |
| Coherence | How well does it fit? | Fit with other interventions in the same institution (internal) and with other actors (external): synergy, complementarity, avoiding duplication. |
| Effectiveness | Is it achieving its objectives? | Includes **differential results across groups**; weigh the relative importance of objectives. Closer to attributable results than impact. |
| Efficiency | How well are resources used? | Economic and **timely**; compared with feasible alternatives; includes operational efficiency. |
| Impact | What difference does it make? | Significant higher-level effects, positive or negative, intended or unintended. |
| Sustainability | Will the benefits last? | Net benefits continue; financial, economic, social, environmental and institutional capacity; resilience, risks, trade-offs. |

Principles for use (para 15): apply thoughtfully and in context (the
evaluation questions and the intended use shape how criteria are read);
do not apply mechanically, because the main complaint in the consultation
was covering too many criteria and questions (para 6). Footnote 4 says an
**evaluability assessment** before an evaluation helps set realistic
expectations. Box 1 says "intervention" includes projects, programmes,
policies, strategies, institutions and instruments, and that the criteria
apply to private sector, non-government and domestic government actors,
not only aid.

### 2.2 Results frameworks: DFID logframe (2011)

Read in part. A logframe has **impact, outcome, outputs** (and activities
or inputs), each with **indicator, baseline, milestones, target, source**,
and **assumptions** (stated at outcome and output level), with an impact
weighting and a risk rating on outputs (pp. 6 to 12 of the PDF; the
summary of changes near the start). Points that bear on this brief:

- It "comes into play at a very early stage" and is used from options
  analysis through to ex-post evaluation (Logframe and the Development
  Cycle).
- "All projects should have baseline data at all levels before they are
  approved." There is a narrow exception: justified in the business case,
  with a plan to obtain it shortly after the start. First check existing
  sources before commissioning new data (baselines).
- **Milestones** act as an early-warning system: they say how the
  project is progressing along the predicted path (indicators section).
- **Planned and achieved results sit side by side**, and the project team
  should define at design what over- and under-achievement would look
  like, because scoring at review uses that comparison (Assessing Impact,
  p. 23).
- **Changes to the logframe** are normal but controlled by level: activity
  changes by the project officer, output changes by the programme manager,
  changes to the impact or outcome statement at the level that approved
  the original case, and changes to indicators, baselines, milestones or
  targets at those two levels by a more senior role (Making changes,
  p. 22). Consultation upwards and downwards is expected.
- The logframe is a thinking tool as much as a form: it helps get
  stakeholder consensus and surfaces problems in the logic or gaps in
  baseline information before the project is presented as viable.

### 2.3 Logic models and theory of change in philanthropy: Kellogg, NPC

- **Kellogg** (read in part): a logic model links resources, activities,
  outputs, outcomes and impact, and the theory and assumptions behind
  them. It is read as "if... then" statements. It says most of the value
  is in the process of creating and validating the model; it is used for
  planning, implementation and evaluation (introduction; "The What").
  Kellogg attaches fixed year bands to its outcome levels. That is a
  choice for its own grantmaking, and I do not carry it over (the rules
  forbid durations as rules).
- **NPC four pillar approach** (read): map your theory of change; prioritise
  what you measure; choose the level of evidence that suits stakeholders
  (a funder may want a certain rigour, academics or government may want a
  counterfactual); select sources and tools, reusing what others measure.
  It says this needs buy-in from leaders and a learning culture, and that
  you should use the findings to improve. The small-charities briefing
  adds proportion: focus effort where limited resources are best used
  (Keeping it in proportion).
- **Charity Commission** (read): trustees' annual reports must state
  objectives and activities, give a summary of **achievements and
  performance** (how the work made a difference to beneficiaries and any
  wider benefit), and, under the newer SORP headings, plans for future
  periods. Smaller charities can keep it brief. This is a statutory
  reporting duty that is outcome-shaped.

### 2.4 Impact investing: Impact Management Project

The five dimensions are **what** outcome and how important it is, **who**
experiences it (and how outcomes differ across groups), **how much**
(scale, depth, duration), **contribution** (whether the effort led to
outcomes likely better than would otherwise have occurred) and **risk**
(the likelihood that impact differs from what is expected). Impact is "a
change in an outcome caused by an organization", positive or negative,
intended or unintended. This is the same shape as the OECD criteria and
the Magenta Book questions in another sector's words. **Contribution** is
the counterfactual idea, and **risk** is the assumption idea, stated as a
dimension of the claim.

### 2.5 Corporate and agile: OKRs and Scrum

- **OKRs** (Google re:Work, read): key results should describe outcomes,
  not activities; they should be measurable and gradable; the "sweet
  spot" for grading is below full attainment, because consistently
  hitting every OKR means they are not ambitious enough; low grades are
  "data" to refine the next OKRs; they should be public inside the
  organisation; they are not a to-do list; check in during the period.
  The ambition stance differs from the government sources (see option O3
  below).
- **Scrum** (read): built on empiricism. Three pillars: **transparency**
  (work is visible to those doing and receiving it), **inspection**
  (progress toward agreed goals is checked often), **adaptation**
  (adjust as soon as the deviation is seen). "Inspection without
  adaptation is considered pointless." The Sprint Review is a working
  session where stakeholders and the team look at what was done and what
  has changed, and decide what to do next; the Product Goal is the single
  long-term objective. This is the agile name for monitoring that feeds
  decisions on a short cycle.
- **SAFe Lean Portfolio Management** (search summary only): strategic
  themes with OKRs, lean budgets for value streams, guardrails, and
  epics validated with minimum viable products before commitment, pivot
  or stop on data. The same test-and-learn logic as the Magenta annex.

### 2.6 US federal: OMB M-19-23 (Evidence Act)

Read. Four components of evidence, "interdependent": **foundational fact
finding, policy analysis, program evaluation and performance
measurement** (Appendix A). Performance measurement is therefore one
fourth of "evidence", not all of it. Agencies must keep a multi-year
**learning agenda**: a systematic plan of **priority questions**
(strategic and operational) with how they will be answered, the data
needed, the methods, and the consultation with stakeholders, "iterative,
flexible, transparent" (Appendix B). An **Evaluation Officer** leads it.
An **annual evaluation plan** lists the year's "significant" evaluations
and their key questions, and the agency defines "significant" by criteria
such as importance to the mission, size, and the knowledge gap the study
fills (Appendix D and its footnote 21). **Capacity assessments** ask
whether the organisation can do this work. Smaller units are "strongly
encouraged" to have their own agenda tied to the agency's, with no single
format. This is the clearest example of an **organisation-level list of
questions that drives which evaluations happen and which data is
collected**.

### 2.7 European Union

The Commission's page (read) says evaluation checks whether a law, policy
or programme is fit for purpose against five criteria: effectiveness,
efficiency, relevance, coherence and **EU added value** (what goes beyond
what member states would achieve alone). It publishes an **annual plan**
of evaluations in its work programme, invites stakeholder input, has an
independent **Regulatory Scrutiny Board** give opinions on the quality of
selected evaluations, and publishes the reports. A search summary adds
that the guidelines use an "evaluate first" principle (evidence from
evaluation before a revision), and that an employers' federation
criticises uneven application. I did not read the guidelines.
Horizon Europe (search summary only) asks proposals to describe **pathways
to impact**: how outputs lead to outcomes and wider impacts, with KPIs and
targets. Treat that as unverified.

### 2.8 IATI (international aid transparency)

Read. A published activity may carry a **result** typed output, outcome
or impact (type code list not confirmed), with **indicators**, each with
an optional **baseline** (date and value), and **periods** each with
start, end, **target** and **actual**, plus **dimension** (breakdown, such
as sex or age) and a flag for whether it is suitable to aggregate. So a
shared standard for results already assumes: baseline, per-period targets,
actuals against them, and breakdowns, and expects them to be published in
a machine-readable way.

### 2.9 Canada, New Zealand, World Bank, WHO: what I could and could not confirm

- **Canada.** Search summary only: the Policy on Results (in effect since
  2016) requires departments to keep a Departmental Results Framework (core
  responsibilities, results, indicators) and a Program Inventory; the
  Directive requires an annual departmental evaluation plan and has
  evaluations focus on relevance, effectiveness and efficiency, with a
  risk-based approach for some spending. I could not read it. Treat as
  unverified.
- **New Zealand Better Business Cases.** Search summary: five-case model
  refreshed March 2026. No verified content on evaluation. The five-case
  structure is the same as the UK's, so it adds no independent evidence.
- **World Bank.** Search summary of example project documents: results
  frameworks have project development objective indicators and
  intermediate results indicators, each with unit, data source, frequency,
  responsible party, baseline and yearly targets; some targets are "to be
  determined" until a baseline study. Nothing at source.
- **WHO.** Search summary: the 2013 handbook has an evaluation planning
  chapter (questions, terms of reference, methodology, resources,
  workplan), and WHO requires a formal **management response** to
  evaluations with tracking of follow-up. A 2023 guide updates it. Not
  read.
- **Standards bodies (ISO 21500 family, PM2, PMI, PRINCE2/MSP).** I could
  not read these. The project-management standards cited in TAXONOMY.md
  and in round two (benefits management, progressive elaboration) are the
  ones already in the repo; I add no new claims about them here.

1. **Rigour of attribution.** The Magenta Book, the Green Book and the

   inspection with the team itself.

## 5. Options where the frameworks differ or leave room

**O1. Where the theory of change lives.**
- (a) Only on a programme (Cartograph today, TAXONOMY D15: "a results
  framework sits at project level and a theory of change at programme
  level").
- (b) On any programme or project: a stand-alone project can carry a short
  chain, which a programme can later gather up.
- (c) A separate record that programmes and projects point to.

The Magenta Book, OECD and DFID all apply the idea to a project. The
split in D15 is from EC and UN practice. Trade-off: (a) keeps one place
and avoids duplicate theories, but the most common small user has one
project and no programme, so the strongest evaluation-by-design tool is
out of reach. (c) is cleanest but adds a noun. **Recommendation (b)**,
because T8 is essential and it needs no new kind: a project's success
criteria already carry `assumes`. Decide in TAXONOMY first.

**O2. How to type a question.** Magenta (process, impact, value for money)
or OECD (six criteria) or Canada (three issues, summary only). The Magenta
types sort questions by what method answers them; the OECD criteria sort
them by what a good intervention should be. They overlap but are not the
same (relevance and coherence have no Magenta type). Trade-off: one
label set is simpler. **Recommendation**: question text is free, with an
optional tag from either list, because bodies differ and the owner wants
a domain-neutral tool.

**O3. What a target means.** Commitment (Green Book, DFID: scored against)
or stretch (OKR: below full attainment is healthy). Trade-off: a stretch
target in a government tool reads as under-delivery; a commitment in a
start-up tool reads as sandbagging. This is my reading from the sources,
not something any one of them says. **Option**: let a target carry a
plain label, commitment or stretch, and have charts and checks treat them
differently. Desirable, not essential.

**O4. Where questions and learning live.**
- (a) Fields on programme and project.
- (b) A new light kind, "Question", at any level, with the decision it
  informs, who answers, and dated answers (like readings, TAXONOMY D8).
- (c) Nothing: leave learning to comments and events.

The US learning agenda and the UK evaluation plan are organisation-level
lists, so a field on one project cannot be a list of the organisation's
questions. Trade-off: a new noun conflicts with "lean core" (D4 refused a
Benefit kind). But D4 also said "readings against existing KPIs, not a new
noun", and the same argument may not hold here: a question is not
a measure. **Recommendation (b)**, switched off by default in the walk
(P2), and decided in TAXONOMY before any build.

**O5. How much to ask for proportionality.** (a) Nothing; (b) a single
choice, "how much checking later" with three plain options and a reason;
(c) the full set of priority criteria. **Recommendation (b)**, because
Magenta Figure 1.3 itself says the first ask at the strategic stage is a
decision on scale.

**O6. Counterfactual.** (a) Not mentioned; (b) one sentence, "what happens
if we do nothing"; (c) a structured comparison design. **Recommendation
(b)** for everyone; (c) only in the full workspace, for people who run
impact evaluations. The Green Book calls this BAU and requires it for any
appraisal; a hobbyist can still write one line.

**O7. Independence.** Government wants independent scrutiny; agile teams
inspect themselves. **Recommendation**: record who checks, never require
independence; a large public body can set a policy to require it.

## 6. Cartograph today against these sources

I read: `docs/TAXONOMY.md` (the definitions, D1 to D12, D13, D14 to D22,
D25, D26, D52, D58, D59, parts of others), the JSON schemas for programme,
project (property lists and key descriptions), KPI, KPIReadings, Gap,
Assumption, PortfolioDecisions and common (Target, Event, Timing), the
list of flows, and in the interface `src/copy.ts` by search for evaluation
words and the portfolio and programme copy, plus the route list. I did
**not** audit the interface screen by screen for this brief, and I did
not open the flows' step lists.

### 6.1 What is already strong and should be kept

1. **A programme's pathway is a real theory of change.** Each step names
   the outcome, what must hold before it, a reason (`because`) and the
   assumptions (`assumes`), and several preconditions are an "and"
   (`programme.schema.json` pathway; TAXONOMY D13). This is rare in
   tools and matches Magenta 2.2.1, Green Book 4.9 to 4.12 and Test and
   Learn. Note that TAXONOMY D12 still says "open" and says the causal
   logic is absent; the pathway exists now, so D12's status needs
   updating.
2. **Assumption is its own kind** with `statement`, `confidence`, `ifFalse`
   and `testedBy` ("an assumption is tested, where a risk is
   mitigated"). That is the Test and Learn idea nearly whole (T2) and
   more specific than any logframe.
3. **The indicator carries the measurement apparatus** in D1: definition,
   unit, direction, sources, cycle, owner (a role), baseline-or-admitted-
   unknown, target (value and date, a window, an event, or "set when"),
   `resultLevel` (output, outcome, impact), breakdowns by Segment, limits.
   This is DFID, IATI and Magenta Figure 4.1 in one record. The admitted
   unknown for a baseline is better than any source I read.
4. **Readings are their own record** (D8) with `provisional` and `note`,
   a restated figure keeps the one it replaced, and a period can be read
   as it stood on a date. That is the audit trail Magenta 5.8 asks for,
   built into storage.
5. **Change sets and versions** give the time-stamped plan and recorded
   amendments of Magenta 2.3.3 and 5.8 for free (G2), and promotion
   policies can express DFID's approval-by-level (G3).
6. **Events with causes** (D52, D59) record what happened and what it
   reached. This is monitoring tied to the plan.
7. **PortfolioDecisions** (invest, hold, stop, with reason and date) is a
   decision record, which is what Q6 needs, though see 6.2.
8. **The Gap kind** with current and desired states measured by a KPI is a
   good form of the problem statement the Green Book calls the case for
   change, and links to relevance in the OECD sense.
9. **People are never definitions, roles are** (D27), which matches
   evaluation guidance on responsibility without hard-wiring a person.

### 6.2 What is missing, as the sources see it

| Gap | What the sources would expect | Where it bites |

### 6.3 What the sources make look irrelevant or misplaced

I try to be candid, not harsh. These are judgments from this topic only; other briefs (G1, G3 to G6) may weigh them differently.

1. **Statistical process control and DMAIC (D58, the `/control` chart,
   Cp/Cpk) are specific to process-improvement work.** None of the
   evaluation sources asks for them. They are valuable where a team
   improves a repeatable process (operations, manufacturing, health
   quality improvement) and noise for a cooperative or NGO programme. The
   current design already keeps them optional ("asks nothing of a project
   not being improved this way"). I would keep them as a domain module,
   not as part of the core of "how we check our work".
2. **Much of the project schema is delivery planning** (procurement,
   responsibilities, triggerable chains, cost lines). The evaluation
   sources ask for very little of this. They ask for the theory, the
   measures, the questions and the decisions. This is not an argument to
   remove it, since the owner wants charters, but it is evidence about
   where the centre of value is: the change and how it will be checked,
   not the plan of work. (Whether planning detail is "wrong centre" is
   for G3 and G5; I only say the evaluation lens does not require it.)
3. **PortfolioDecisions as a single latest-state file** is a record of
   *state*, where the sources want a record of *decision plus evidence*.
   It is a good start and probably misplaced as a separate file if
   decisions accumulate (the version history may already hold the past,
   which I did not verify).
4. **SMART marks (D25, D26)** are right: the Green Book requires SMART
   objectives (4.21). I found nothing irrelevant there. The "Attainable"
   reading (every measure has today's figure, dated before its target)
   is a mechanical stand-in for what the Green Book means by achievable,
   but a sensible one.
5. **Benefit kind refused (D4)** is consistent with the sources if
   "readings against existing KPIs" stays. The UK benefits guide (round
   two) wants one named owner per benefit; Cartograph has an owner role on
   KPI. Nothing irrelevant, but a benefit is rarely one KPI. Keep D4;
   watch it.

### 6.4 Is a rebuild needed, on this topic?

Plainly: **no, not for evaluation by design.** The contract already holds
the hardest parts (a theory of change with reasons and assumptions,
assumptions that can be tested, indicators with baselines or admitted
unknowns, readings with history, events with causes). The missing parts
are small additions in the contract: a question, a decision-from-evidence
link, a proportionality choice, a one-line "if nothing is done", interim
targets, a review at closing. The larger change is in **order and
emphasis**: the sources put the change and the question first and the
work plan after, while the project-centred charter walk starts from the
work. D55 ("the charter is a brief") suggests the engine already treats
the charter as a rendering, which is consistent with a different centre.
Whether the interface should start every walk from "what change, and how
will you know", is for G4 and G6 to weigh with the user tests; the
evaluation sources support it.

## 7. Diátaxis lens

- **Tutorial (someone learning).** The walk. Evaluation sources ask for
  little at this stage (P2): one change statement, one measure, one
  decision it informs. The Magenta Book itself puts the first ask at a
  "decision on scale". Do not teach a theory-of-change method here.
- **How-to (someone doing a task).** "Add a question and say what it
  informs." "Record what we decided after the readings." "Close a
  project and note what we learned." "Mark which assumption to test
  first." These are task pages in the workspace.
- **Reference (someone looking something up).** Field and kind
  definitions; the six criteria and the three question types as a
  glossary table; the OECD definitions paraphrased with links.
- **Explanation (someone trying to understand).** Why check later, why
  proportion, what a counterfactual is for, why a low score can be data
  (OKR), why inspection without adaptation is pointless (Scrum). One
  short optional page, cited inline, per Diátaxis. Keep it out of field
  hints.

## 8. Short list of what I would carry forward

1. Make the change, the assumptions and the question the first things
   asked, and the plan of work second (T1, T2, Q1, Q2, P2).
2. Make the pathway available to a stand-alone project (T8, O1).
3. Add a light Question record and a link from a decision to the evidence
   behind it (Q2, Q6; O4). Decide it in TAXONOMY first.
4. Add one proportionality choice and one "if nothing is done" sentence
   (P1, T5).
5. Add interim targets or milestones to indicators (D3).
6. Add a closing review (what held, what did not, what lasts) (L1, L2).
7. Add criticality and evidence strength to assumptions and a "test these
   first" view (T3).
8. Keep DMAIC and control charts as an optional module, not core (6.3).
9. Treat the OECD criteria and Magenta types as optional lenses, not
   required fields (L4, Q4, O2).

## Open questions

1. **Canada, New Zealand, World Bank, WHO, Horizon Europe, SAFe, ISO
   21500/21502, PM2, NHS, UKRI, RIBA's use stage, Balanced Scorecard,
   IRIS+** were not read at source. The "domains support" columns for these
   rest on search summaries or are blank. Someone with access should read
   them before the criteria are final, especially the Canadian Directive
   on Results and the NZ Better Business Cases monitoring guidance, which
   are the two non-UK government equivalents the owner asked about.
2. **EU Better Regulation Guidelines and Toolbox** (tools on evaluation):
   not read; the "evaluate first" principle is from search results.
3. **Magenta Book Annex A and the Transparency annex** were not read. They
   define cost-effectiveness and cost-benefit methods and the formal
   pre-registration practice. Criteria G2 and Q4 may need adjustment.
4. **Does the walk already ask about proportionality or lessons?** I
   searched `copy.ts` for evaluation words and read the portfolio and
   programme copy, but I did not walk the project flow screens. The
   "missing" list in 6.2 is therefore from the contract, not from the
   interface.
5. **Does PortfolioDecisions keep history through versions?** If so, the
   point in 6.3 item 3 is weaker.
6. **Where should a stakeholder achievement report come from?** The
   Charity Commission reporting duty (objectives, activities,
   achievements, plans) looks like a rendering of existing records. I did
   not check whether the engine already renders it.
7. **Targets as commitment or stretch (O3)** is my inference from two
   culture clusters of sources, not a finding of any one source. It needs
   an owner decision.
8. **Interim targets (D3):** the contract's `Target` allows a value by a
   date or an event, but I did not check whether interim values can be
   held through readings or milestones elsewhere (a KPI's cycle plus a
   project milestone may already stand in).
9. **The Kellogg guide's fixed year bands** were left out of the criteria on purpose
   (rules on sizes and durations). Whether any similar default is useful
   for new users is a product question.
